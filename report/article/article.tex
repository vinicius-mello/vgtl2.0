\begin{abstract}
A smooth plane algebraic curve is a compact Riemann surface and a real
surface of codimension $2$ in $\CP^2$. Most methods that mesh it work in
an affine chart or over a branched covering of $\CP^1$, and must
therefore treat points at infinity, charts or branch cuts separately. We
triangulate the curve directly inside $\CP^2$, with no distinguished
chart. A $15$-vertex triangulation of $\CP^2$, due to Gaifullin, is
refined adaptively by Maubach bisection along Fubini--Study geodesics,
and the curve is followed from cell to cell by continuation. In each
cell, the curve meets the triangular $2$-faces in isolated points, which
are joined into polygons. Realizing every face as a geodesic cone,
rather than as a flat triangle in an affine chart, makes the faces shared
by neighboring cells coincide exactly, so the pieces glue into a closed
surface, whose topology is checked a posteriori. On our test curves at
depth $14$, fewer than $0.14\%$ of the cells that meet the curve fail, against
$1.8$--$11.6\%$ with chart-flat faces; the area agrees with Wirtinger's
theorem to within $0.15\%$; and, unlike a box in an affine chart at equal
resolution, the mesh covers the whole curve.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

Let $F(X,Y,Z)$ be a homogeneous polynomial of degree $n$ such that
$C=\{F=0\}\subset\CP^2$ is smooth. Then $C$ is a compact Riemann surface of
genus $g=(n-1)(n-2)/2$, and, as a real object, a closed orientable surface
of codimension $2$ in the real $4$-manifold $\CP^2$. This paper is about
computing a triangulation of $C$, a simplicial surface close to $C$ with
the right topology, for visualization and as a combinatorial model.

Existing methods each treat some part of $C$ specially.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Affine charts.} Meshing $\{F(x,y,1)=0\}$ in a grid of
  $\C^2=\R^4$~\cite{weiglebanks1996} loses the $n$ points at infinity, and
  in a bounded box it loses a neighborhood of them. Covering $\CP^2$ needs
  three charts, whose meshes must then be stitched along the overlaps.
\item \emph{Branched coverings.} Viewing $C$ as an $n$-sheeted covering of
  $\CP^1$~\cite{deconinckvanhoeij2001,nieser2010} requires the branch
  points, cuts between them, and a separate treatment of the fiber over
  $\infty$.
\item \emph{Other compactifications.} Embedding $\C^2$ in
  $\CP^1\times\CP^1$ instead of $\CP^2$ can create singularities that the
  curve does not have: the closure of $w^2=z^3-z$ has a cusp at
  $(\infty,\infty)$ in $\CP^1\times\CP^1$, while its closure
  $X^2Z=Y^3-YZ^2$ in $\CP^2$ is smooth. On the same code, extraction
  failures concentrate at that cusp (\Cref{sec:exp-s2s2}).
\end{itemize}
In $\CP^2$ the smooth curve is a smooth compact submanifold, with no
distinguished points. A method that works intrinsically in $\CP^2$ needs
none of these special cases.

\paragraph{Approach.}
We triangulate $\CP^2$ itself and refine it adaptively around $C$.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item The seed is Gaifullin's $15$-vertex triangulation of
  $\CP^2$~\cite{gaifullin2009}. It is balanced, and therefore a valid
  initial mesh for Maubach bisection (\Cref{sec:mesh}).
\item Refinement is Maubach bisection~\cite{maubach1995,traxler1997},
  with new vertices at Fubini--Study geodesic midpoints.
\item The mesh is pointerless: a cell is a seed index plus an LPT
  code~\cite{atalaymount2007}, and neighbors are computed, not stored
  (\Cref{sec:lpt}).
\item The curve is followed by continuation. In each cell, the points
  where it meets the triangular $2$-faces are found by Newton's method
  and joined into polygons; each face is realized as a geodesic cone,
  so that faces glue exactly along their edges (\Cref{sec:extraction}).
\item The topology of the result is checked, and holes are closed
  (\Cref{sec:topology}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item An intrinsic pipeline that triangulates smooth plane curves inside
  $\CP^2$, with no charts, cuts or special treatment of infinity, built
  from Maubach bisection on a balanced seed of $\CP^2$, codimension-$2$
  extraction and combinatorial continuation.
\item An extension of LPT codes from the Kuhn cube to arbitrary balanced
  seeds, including seeds that triangulate closed manifolds. The neighbor
  procedure is fully combinatorial and table-driven, and it is verified
  exactly. We also compare its memory use with an explicit incidence
  structure.
\item A chart-free realization of the triangular faces as Fubini--Study
  geodesic cones. Faces then glue exactly along geodesic edges, so the
  boundary of every tetrahedral facet is a closed surface and meets $C$ an
  even number of times. Compared with faces that are flat in affine
  charts, this cuts extraction failures by a factor of $26$ to $175$ on our
  test curves.
\item An a-posteriori topological check and a closing step that turn an
  uncertified extraction into a closed surface of the right Euler
  characteristic, together with a global accuracy check by Wirtinger's
  theorem.
\item An experimental comparison with the affine-chart approach on the
  same code base, at equal Fubini--Study resolution: a box in a chart
  misses neighbourhoods of the points at infinity, its missing area
  decreasing only like $1/R^2$ with its half-width $R$, and at $R=16$ it
  already uses more cells than the whole of $\CP^2$.
\item A comparison with $\CP^1\times\CP^1$ on the same code base, whose
  balanced seed is the product of two octahedra. Where this
  compactification gives the curve a cusp, extraction failures concentrate
  at it, and some polygons cut across it with the wrong orientation. A
  test with the Kähler form, $|\omega|/\mathrm{area}$ per polygon, detects
  them, after which the closed surface has the right topology.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Computing with compact Riemann surfaces.}
Algebraic curves are usually handled numerically as branched coverings
of $\CP^1$, to compute monodromy, periods and related invariants~\cite{deconinckvanhoeij2001,bobenkoklein2013}.
Visualizations project the curve into a chart: Hanson's Fermat
surfaces~\cite{hanson1994} use an explicit parametrization;
Nieser, Poelke and Polthier~\cite{nieser2010} mesh the multivalued graph
over $\C$ from the branch points; Kranich~\cite{kranich2016} colors
domains on the GPU; and Dutter's chart-free map~\cite{dutter2026}
displays the whole of $\CP^2$ in a bounded region of $\R^3$.
Bertini real~\cite{bertinireal2017} computes, by homotopy continuation,
a cell decomposition of the real points of a complex algebraic set of
dimension one or two. Writing $F=0$ as the two real equations
$\mathrm{Re}\,F=\mathrm{Im}\,F=0$ in an affine chart makes the curve a real
surface in $\R^4$ to which it applies, but in one chart at a time.

\paragraph{Implicit manifolds of codimension two.}
Our extraction relates to piecewise-linear approximation of implicit
manifolds over Freudenthal--Kuhn
triangulations~\cite{allgowerschmidt1985,allgowergeorg2003}, to its
certified, uniform-grid variant by cell-to-cell
tracing~\cite{bkw2023,boissonnatwintraecken2022}, and most directly to
the extraction of zero sets of functions $\C^2\to\C$ on uniform grids of
$\R^4$~\cite{weiglebanks1996}, which is the baseline of
\Cref{sec:exp-affine}. Isosurface extraction over bisection hierarchies
is common in codimension one~\cite{gerstnerrumpf2000,pascucci2002};
adaptive simplicial grids for implicit complexes in $\R^3$, including
curve networks, are recent~\cite{ju2024}. Castelo et al.~\cite{castelo2006} triangulate implicit
manifolds adaptively in any dimension with the J1a triangulation, and
Bhaniramka, Wenger and Crawfis~\cite{bwc2004} extract isosurfaces in any
dimension with guarantees on the output, in codimension one.

\paragraph{Certification.}
Interval and Bernstein subdivision certify the topology of implicit
curves and surfaces~\cite{plantingavegter2004,mourrainpavone2009};
on simplices, barycentric Bernstein bounds play the same
role~\cite{reuter2008}. Our method is not certified: it reports the cells where extraction fails and checks the
topology of the result a posteriori.

\paragraph{Bisection and initial meshes.}
Newest-vertex bisection goes back to Mitchell~\cite{mitchell1988,mitchell1991}
in 2D, and was extended to arbitrary dimension by
Maubach~\cite{maubach1995} and Traxler~\cite{traxler1997}. Its weak point
is the initial mesh, which must carry compatible vertex orders. Remedies
include pre-refinement~\cite{kossaczky1994,stevenson2008}, renumbering~\cite{agk2018},
marked-bisection stages~\cite{belda2023}, and the coloring-based
initialization of Diening, Gehring and Storn~\cite{dgs2025}. All of them
concern domains in $\R^d$. We use a seed of a closed manifold, which is
admissible because it is balanced
(\Cref{prop:balanced}).

\paragraph{Pointerless hierarchical meshes.}
Several representations encode a simplex of a regular bisection hierarchy
by a label instead of pointers: Hebert~\cite{hebert1994}; Evans,
Kirkpatrick and Townsend~\cite{ekt2001}; Lee, De Floriani and
Samet~\cite{ldfs2001}; Maubach's own neighbor location~\cite{maubach1996};
and Atalay and Mount's LPT codes~\cite{atalaymount2007} in arbitrary
dimension, which we previously used for adaptive cellular structures in
3D printing~\cite{sa2015adaptive}. Weiss and De Floriani use the star of
the bisection edge (a \emph{diamond}) as primitive~\cite{weissdefloriani2011};
Dupuy's concurrent binary trees implement longest-edge bisection on the
GPU~\cite{dupuy2020}; Boissonnat, Kachanovich and Wintraecken encode
Freudenthal--Kuhn simplices compactly, without adaptivity~\cite{bkw2023}.
Architecturally closest to our construction are the forests of
p4est~\cite{p4est2011} and t8code~\cite{bursteddeholke2016}: an arbitrary
coarse mesh refined tree by tree, with a per-tree linear code and an
inter-tree connectivity table. Those forests are non-conforming (hanging
nodes, red refinement, $d\le3$); ours is a conforming, graded bisection
forest in arbitrary dimension.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The Fubini--Study geometry of \texorpdfstring{$\CP^2$}{CP2}}

We write points of $\CP^2$ as unit vectors $p\in\C^3$, defined up to a
phase, with the Hermitian product $\langle p,q\rangle=\sum_k p_k\bar q_k$
(see~\cite[chap.~4]{bengtssonzyczkowski2017} for the facts used in this
subsection). The Fubini--Study distance is
\[
  d(p,q)=\arccos\,|\langle p,q\rangle|\in[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}].
\]
Linear combinations of representatives are not well defined on $\CP^2$:
$q$ and $-q$ are the same point, yet $(p+q)/2$ and $(p-q)/2$ are
different points, and the second vanishes when $q=p$. So, to average two
points, we first align their phases. If $\langle p,q\rangle\ne0$, that
is, if $d(p,q)<\frac{\pi}{2}$, the representative of $q$
\emph{aligned to $p$} is
\[
  \tilde q=e^{i\theta}q,\qquad
  e^{i\theta}=\frac{\overline{\langle q,p\rangle}}{|\langle q,p\rangle|},
\]
the unique representative of the point $q$ with $\langle\tilde q,p\rangle$
real and positive. It depends only on the point $q$, not on the vector
chosen for it, and replacing $p$ by $e^{i\alpha}p$ replaces $\tilde q$ by
$e^{i\alpha}\tilde q$. Hence $[(1-u)p+u\tilde q]$ depends only on the two
points and on $u$. For an aligned pair,
the points $[(1-u)p+uq]$, $u\in[0,1]$, trace the minimizing geodesic from
$p$ to $q$, since the real plane spanned by $p$ and $q$ is orthogonal to
the fibers of $\C^3\setminus\{0\}\to\CP^2$: geodesics of $\CP^2$ are the
projections of horizontal great circles of the unit sphere
$S^5$~\cite{bengtssonzyczkowski2017}. We call $u$ the \emph{linear
parameter} of the geodesic. The geodesic midpoint is its point $u=\frac12$,
$(p+q)/\|p+q\|$. More generally, we call the normalized sum of
phase-aligned representatives (aligned to the first point) the
\emph{barycenter} of a set of nearby points; we use it only to place the
apices of the closing cones (\Cref{sec:topology}).

The affine chart $U_k=\{p_k\ne0\}$ has coordinates $p_i/p_k$, $i\ne k$.
For a simplex we use its \emph{best-conditioned chart}, the $k$ that
maximizes $\min_v|p_k(v)|$ over its vertices $v$. In this chart all its
vertices are far from the line at infinity $\{p_k=0\}$.

Let $C$ be a smooth curve of degree $n$. By Wirtinger's theorem, the area
of $C$ in the Fubini--Study metric is $n$ times the area of a projective
line~\cite{griffithsharris1978}. With the distance above, a projective
line is a round sphere of radius $\frac12$, so $\area(C)=n\pi$. This gives an exact global quantity to
compare with the area of a computed triangulation: we use it to measure
accuracy, and coverage in \Cref{sec:exp-affine}.

\subsection{Maubach bisection}

A cell of a $d$-mesh is an ordered simplex
$\sigma=\langle v_0,\dots,v_d\rangle$ with a level $\level(\sigma)\ge0$.
Maubach's rule~\cite{maubach1995} sets
$\type(\sigma)=d-(\level(\sigma)\bmod d)$, bisects the edge
$v_0v_{\type(\sigma)}$ at a new vertex $v$, and replaces $\sigma$ by the
two children
\[
  \langle v_0,\dots,v_{l-1},v,v_{l+1},\dots,v_d\rangle,\qquad
  \langle v_1,\dots,v_l,v,v_{l+1},\dots,v_d\rangle,\qquad l=\type(\sigma),
\]
both of level $\level(\sigma)+1$. To keep the mesh conforming, a cell is
bisected only together with every other cell of the same level that
contains its split edge, after bisecting recursively any coarser cell
containing that edge. This terminates, and produces conforming meshes,
when the initial mesh is \emph{compatible}~\cite{maubach1995,traxler1997,stevenson2008}.
We use the strongest form of compatibility. An initial mesh is
\emph{strongly compatible} if, whenever two cells share a facet, the
facet omits the vertex at the same position $i$ in both, and the
remaining vertices appear in the same order.

A $d$-complex is \emph{balanced} if its vertices admit a coloring
$\col:V\to\{0,\dots,d\}$ that is injective on every cell.

\begin{proposition}[{\cite{traxler1997}, \cite[\S3.1]{dgs2025}}]\label[proposition]{prop:balanced}
A conforming $d$-mesh admits vertex orders that make it strongly
compatible if and only if it is balanced. The order of each cell is then
the order of the colors of its vertices.
\end{proposition}

Indeed, if cells are ordered by color, the common facet of two adjacent
cells carries every color except the missing one, which occupies the
same position in both. Strong compatibility is a statement about
\emph{ordered} facets: matching only the position of the omitted vertex,
without the order of the rest, is not enough.

\subsection{The seed}
\label{sec:cp2}

$\CP^2$ has two classical small triangulations. Kühnel's
$\CP^2_9$~\cite{kuhnelbanchoff1983} has $9$ vertices and is vertex-minimal.
Gaifullin's $X$~\cite{gaifullin2009} has $15$ vertices, $f$-vector
$(15,90,240,270,108)$, and automorphism group $S_4\times S_3$, which acts
by Fubini--Study isometries in Gaifullin's explicit realization.
Gaifullin shows that $X$ is balanced: color the three vertices of
$V_4\setminus\{e\}$ with $0$ and the vertices $(a,b)$ with $a$, for
$a=1,\dots,4$. He also shows that $X$ is vertex-minimal among balanced
triangulations of $\CP^2$. By \Cref{prop:balanced}, $X$ ordered by its
coloring is a strongly compatible seed. Kühnel's $\CP^2_9$ is not: it is
$3$-neighborly, so its graph is $K_9$, which is not $5$-colorable.

\begin{remark}
We verified these facts independently on the facet list of $X$ (with
\texttt{simpcomp}~\cite{simpcomp}):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item every vertex has exactly two non-neighbors, namely the other members
  of its color class;
\item all $240$ triangles have degree $4$ or $6$;
\item in the color order, all $270$ shared facets omit the vertex at the
  same position on both sides.
\end{itemize}
The $108$ seed cells are also geometrically uniform: the max/min
Fubini--Study edge ratio is $1.4636$ in every cell, as expected from the
isometric symmetry. The barycentric subdivision of $\CP^2_9$ is balanced
too, but in Kühnel's standard realization several flag barycenters
coincide with existing vertices, which produces degenerate cells.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Geometry.}
We use Gaifullin's coordinates~\cite{gaifullin2009}, moved by a random
unitary change of basis of $\C^3$ to avoid special alignments between the
curve and the seed. Each new vertex is placed at the Fubini--Study
geodesic midpoint of the split edge, after aligning the phases of the two
homogeneous representatives. The coordinates of a cell's vertices are
not stored: they are recomputed from the seed along the cell's path in
the hierarchy, deterministically, so every cell sees the same point for
the same vertex.

\subsection{Pointerless codes on balanced seeds}
\label{sec:lpt}

\paragraph{Why codes.}
An explicit mesh stores each simplex with references to its faces and
cofaces. The number of faces per cell grows quickly with the dimension. In
the Freudenthal--Kuhn triangulation of $\R^d$, which is the combinatorial
type of uniform Maubach refinements of the cube, a $k$-simplex is
determined up to translation by an ordered sequence of $k$ disjoint
nonempty subsets of $\{1,\dots,d\}$. There are therefore
$k!\,S(d{+}1,k{+}1)/d!$ of them per $d$-cell, where $S$ denotes Stirling
numbers of the second kind. A full incidence structure storing only the
boundary references ($k+1$ integers per $k$-simplex) already needs:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
$d$ & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8 \\
\midrule
simplices of dim.\ $1..d$ per cell & 2.5 & 4.2 & 6.2 & 9.0 & 13.0 & 18.8 & 27.1 \\
boundary references per cell & 6.0 & 12.3 & 22.5 & 39.0 & 65.7 & 108.3 & 175.8 \\
LPT code bits (40 levels) & 49 & 52 & 55 & 59 & 62 & 66 & 70 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A \emph{compact} explicit structure stores cells only, each with its $d+1$
vertex identifiers and $d+1$ neighbor identifiers, i.e.\ $8(d+1)$ bytes
per cell with 32-bit integers ($40$ bytes for $d=4$) plus bookkeeping for
levels and the hierarchy. An LPT code needs about
$L+\log_2(2^dd!)+O(\log L)$ bits for $L$ levels, whatever the dimension.
\Cref{sec:exp-cost} compares memory in practice.

\paragraph{LPT codes.}
Atalay and Mount~\cite{atalaymount2007} encode the Maubach hierarchy of a
Kuhn-triangulated cube $[-1,1]^d$. Its reference root simplex is
\[
  S_\varnothing=\{x\in\R^d:\ 1\ge x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_d\ge-1\},
\]
whose facet $H_0$ lies on $x_1=1$, facet $H_j$ ($0<j<d$) on $x_j=x_{j+1}$,
and facet $H_d$ on $x_d=-1$. A descendant is encoded by its \emph{LPT
code} $(\ell,\Pi,\Phi)$:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item $\ell$ is the level modulo $d$;
\item $\Pi=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_d)$ is a signed permutation;
\item $\Phi$ is the orthant list. After every $d$ levels, one bit is
  appended to each coordinate. We read these bits as integers
  $o_1,\dots,o_d\in[0,2^r)$, where $r$ is the number of completed rounds
  and the oldest bit is the most significant.
\end{itemize}
Their Theorem~1 gives the vertices explicitly. With $T_j=2^r-1-2o_j$, the
$k$-th vertex is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lpt-vertex}
  x^{(k)}=\frac{T+y^{(k)}}{2^r},\qquad
  y^{(k)}_{|\pi_j|}=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_j)\,v_{kj},\qquad
  v_{kj}=\begin{cases}1,&k\ge j,\\0,&k<\min(j,\ell),\\-1,&\ell\le k<j,\end{cases}
\end{equation}
for $k=0,\dots,d$ and $j=1,\dots,d$. Here $y^{(k)}\in\{-1,0,1\}^d$ depends
only on $(\ell,\Pi,k)$, while $T$ depends only on the orthant list.
Their Theorem~2 computes the code of a facet neighbor by table lookup.
It describes neighbors in the infinite Kuhn arrangement: in the cube, a
neighbor outside one root simplex is another root simplex of the same
cube. Our implementation descends from the one we wrote for the Adaptive
Voids algorithm~\cite{sa2015adaptive}, where LPT codes over the six Kuhn
tetrahedra of a cube drive an adaptive subdivision of a solid.

\paragraph{Balanced seeds.}
Let $K_0$ be a balanced $d$-dimensional seed with $N_0$ cells, each ordered
by color, and identify every seed cell $\tau=\langle\tau[0],\dots,\tau[d]\rangle$
with $S_\varnothing$ by sending the $k$-th vertex of $S_\varnothing$ to
$\tau[k]$. A cell of the refined seed is a pair $(s,\kappa)$: a seed index
$s$ and an LPT code $\kappa$ of the reference hierarchy.

We work \emph{combinatorially}: a vertex of the hierarchy of $\tau_s$ is
identified with its barycentric coordinates $w\in\Delta^d$ with respect to
$\tau_s$. These are dyadic rationals, and by \eqref{eq:lpt-vertex} the
coordinates $w(\kappa,m)$ of the $m$-th vertex of $(s,\kappa)$ depend on
$\kappa$ only. A vertex on a facet of $\tau_s$ is also a vertex of the
neighboring seed cell, via the identification of the common facet. A
geometric realization is applied afterwards: affine, or by Fubini--Study
midpoints. Since it is defined edge by edge from the endpoints of the
split edge, it respects these identifications.

\begin{proposition}[seed-boundary test]\label[proposition]{prop:boundary-test}
Facet $\partial_i$ of the cell with code $\kappa=(\ell,\Pi,\Phi)$ lies on
the facet $H_j$ of its seed cell if and only if:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=1pt]
\item\label{it:parallel} the linear part of the equation of $H_j$ is
  constant on $\{y^{(k)}:k\ne i\}$, i.e.\ $y^{(k)}_1\equiv1$ ($j=0$),
  $y^{(k)}_{j+1}-y^{(k)}_j\equiv2\delta$ with $\delta\in\{-1,0,1\}$
  ($0<j<d$), or $y^{(k)}_d\equiv-1$ ($j=d$); and
\item\label{it:offset} $o_1=0$ ($j=0$), $o_{j+1}-o_j=\delta$ ($0<j<d$), or
  $o_d=2^r-1$ ($j=d$).
\end{enumerate}
At most one $j$ satisfies \ref{it:parallel}, which depends only on
$(\ell,\Pi,i)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substitute \eqref{eq:lpt-vertex} into the equation of $H_j$. For $0<j<d$,
it reads $T_j+y^{(k)}_j=T_{j+1}+y^{(k)}_{j+1}$ for all $k\ne i$. Since $T$
does not depend on $k$, this forces $y^{(k)}_{j+1}-y^{(k)}_j$ to be
constant, equal to $T_j-T_{j+1}=2(o_{j+1}-o_j)$. For $j=0$, the equation
$T_1+y^{(k)}_1=2^r$ gives $2o_1=y^{(k)}_1-1$; since $o_1\ge0$ and
$y^{(k)}_1\le1$, this means $o_1=0$ and $y^{(k)}_1=1$. The case $j=d$ is
symmetric. At most one $j$ qualifies, because the $d$ vertices of a
facet span a hyperplane with a unique normal direction, and the facet
normals of a simplex are pairwise non-parallel.
\end{proof}

Condition~\ref{it:parallel} is precomputed in a table indexed by
$(\ell,\Pi,i)$ that stores $j$ and $\delta$, or ``none''. For $d=4$ the
table has $4\cdot384\cdot5=7\,680$ one-byte entries. The test is then a
single lookup followed by a single integer comparison.

\begin{proposition}[seed crossing]\label[proposition]{prop:crossing}
Suppose facet $\partial_i$ of $c=(s,\kappa)$ lies on the facet
$\partial_j\tau_s$ of its seed cell, and let $s'$ be the seed cell sharing
that facet. Then the neighbor of $c$ across $\partial_ic$ is
$c'=(s',\kappa)$, the same code in the other seed cell, and
$\partial_ic'=\partial_ic$ as ordered simplices.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since the seed is ordered by color, $\partial_j\tau_s=\partial_j\tau_{s'}$
as ordered simplices, i.e.\ $\tau_s[p]=\tau_{s'}[p]$ for every $p\ne j$.
For $m\ne i$, the $m$-th vertex of $c$ lies on $\partial_j\tau_s$, so its
coordinates satisfy $w_j(\kappa,m)=0$. The $m$-th vertex of $c'$ has the
same coordinates with respect to $\tau_{s'}$, hence the same position on
the common facet. It is therefore the same vertex of the refined seed.
So $c'$ contains $\partial_ic$, in the same positions, has the same level
as $c$, and lies in the other seed cell. Uniform refinements of a
strongly compatible mesh are conforming~\cite{maubach1995,traxler1997},
so exactly two cells of that level contain $\partial_ic$. Hence $c'$ is
the neighbor.
\end{proof}

This is where balance, rather than any compatible ordering, is used:
without it the two identifications would differ on the common facet by a
permutation, and the code would have to be transformed.

\paragraph{Neighbor finding.}
To find the neighbor of $c=(s,\kappa)$ across $\partial_ic$:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Sibling facet.} If $\partial_ic$ is the facet created by the
  last bisection ($i=d$ for a $0$-child, $i=\type$ for a $1$-child, in
  the vertex numbering of the codes), return the other child of the
  parent.
\item \emph{Boundary test.} Otherwise, look up $(j,\delta)$ for
  $(\ell,\Pi,i)$ and check the orthant condition of
  \Cref{prop:boundary-test}.
\item \emph{Interior facet.} If the test fails, return $(s,\kappa')$ with
  $\kappa'$ given by Atalay and Mount's Theorem~2.
\item \emph{Seed crossing.} Otherwise return $(\adj[s][j],\kappa)$
  (\Cref{prop:crossing}), where $\adj$ is the $N_0\times(d{+}1)$ seed
  adjacency table. Root cells use this rule directly, with $j=i$.
\end{enumerate}
Rule~2 is indispensable. Across a seed-boundary facet, Atalay and Mount's
formula returns a cell of the reflected reference simplex, i.e.\ one with
negative barycentric weights. Such crossings are frequent: about $24\%$
of the non-sibling facets sampled at realistic depths lie on the seed
boundary. \Cref{fig:seed-crossing} illustrates rules~2 and~4.
Here $0$-child and $1$-child refer to Atalay and Mount's numbering, and
$\type=\ell$ with $\ell=0$ read as $d$.

\paragraph{Verification.}
We checked every returned neighbor \emph{exactly} on Gaifullin's seed
($d=4$). Both cells were written as integer barycentric coordinates over
the $15$ global vertices, with common denominator $2^{11}$, and we
checked that the returned cell is a different cell of the same level
containing the queried facet. The test covered $10^6$ random cells over
the whole representable depth range and five million queries: $977\,070$
sibling, $3\,317\,979$ interior and $704\,951$ seed-crossing. None failed,
and away from sibling facets the facet always sat at the same local index
on both sides. An earlier implementation computed the boundary test from
the full matrix of barycentric weights ($O(d^2+rd)$ integer operations)
and resolved crossings by floating-point point location. The table-based
test agrees with it on $7.8$ million facets, and same-code crossing agrees
on $1.4$ million crossings. The new rules take $4.3$\,ns and $5$\,ns per
query, against $79$\,ns and $211$\,ns.

\paragraph{The leaf set.}
\label{sec:leafset}
The mesh is the set of codes of the current leaves, stored in an
open-addressing hash table: one 64-bit slot per leaf, linear probing,
and backward-shift deletion, which avoids tombstones under the
erase-two-insert churn of bisection. For Gaifullin's seed a code fits in
one word: $7$ bits of seed index, $2$ bits for $\ell$, $9$ for $\Pi$, $4$
for $r$ and $4\times10$ for the orthant list, i.e.\ $62$ bits and a depth of
up to $43$. Two operations are built on the table:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item $\texttt{compat\_bisect}(c)$ is Maubach's conforming bisection on
  codes. It bisects coarser neighbors recursively before bisecting $c$,
  so the mesh stays graded: no facet separates cells more than one level
  apart;
\item $\texttt{neighbor\_leaf}(c,i)$ walks up the ancestor chain of the
  combinatorial neighbor to the current leaf, or reports that the region
  has been refined further.
\end{itemize}
An exhaustive graded-invariant test, with about $40\,000$ cascading
bisections, $4$ million leaves and $11.6$ million facet checks, found no
violation. Vertex identifiers are not part of the code. When needed
(for geometry), a new vertex receives an integer identifier, keyed by the
identifiers of the endpoints of the split edge in a separate hash table;
vertices are about $1/d!$ as numerous as cells.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Enumerating the several finer leaves that border a coarser facet is not
implemented; such queries are reported, never guessed. Seeds that are
compatible but not balanced would need a permutation correction in
\Cref{prop:crossing}. For the initialization of~\cite{dgs2025} with $N>d$
colors, a cell is a face of a virtual $N$-simplex. Refinements of the
virtual extension do not restrict to refinements of the
mesh~\cite[Rem.~12]{dgs2025}, so it is unclear how to encode them.

\subsection{Realizing the faces}
\label{sec:faces}

The mesh is combinatorial; extraction needs every triangular $2$-face as
an actual surface in $\CP^2$, on which $F=0$ can be solved. The obvious
choice, the flat triangle spanned by the three vertices in an affine
chart, depends on the chart. A face shared by many cells is solved once,
so it can use only one chart, and the natural choice is the face's own
best-conditioned chart. But then two faces that share an edge and use
different charts see that edge as two different curves
(\Cref{fig:faces}a). The four faces of a tetrahedral facet no longer
form a closed surface, and the curve can cross the resulting seams an
odd number of times.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/faces.pdf}
\caption{Two realizations of two triangles $ABC$ and $ABD$ that share the
edge $AB$, drawn on a projective line $\CP^1\subset\CP^2$, which in the

through that chart's point at infinity ($\infty_0$, $\infty_1$; dotted),
so the two versions of $AB$ differ and enclose a seam (gray). Here
$|AB|=0.55$, comparable to the cells of depths $12$--$14$, and the two
versions are up to $0.16$ apart. (b)~Geodesic cones: both triangles are
bounded by the same great-circle arc $AB$. Generated by
\texttt{fig\_faces.py}.}
\label{fig:faces}
\end{figure} In our first
implementation this was the dominant source of failures
(\Cref{sec:exp-faces}).

We realize every face without charts. Let $A,B,C$ be its vertices, sorted
by global identifier, with their unit representatives. The face is the
\emph{geodesic cone} from $A$ over the geodesic $BC$:
\[
  q(t)=(1-t)B+t\,\tilde C,\qquad
  v(s,t)=(1-s)A+s\,\tilde q(t),\qquad (s,t)\in[0,1]^2,
\]
where $\tilde C$ is $C$ aligned to $B$ and $\tilde q(t)$ is $q(t)$
aligned to $A$ (\Cref{sec:prelim}). The first alignment makes $q$ the
geodesic $BC$; the second makes each segment $s\mapsto v(s,t)$ the
geodesic from $A$ to $q(t)$. By the invariance of alignment, the point
$[v(s,t)]$ does not depend on the representatives chosen for $A$, $B$,
$C$. On the boundary, $t=0$, $t=1$ and $s=1$ give the
geodesics $AB$, $AC$ and $BC$, each traversed by its linear parameter
from the lower identifier to the higher one. An edge is therefore the
same curve, with the same parameter, in every face that contains it
(\Cref{fig:faces}b), and the Fubini--Study midpoint used for bisection
lies on it. We use
\[
  (\lambda_A,\lambda_B,\lambda_C)=(1-s,\ s(1-t),\ st)
\]
as barycentric coordinates on the face; on each edge they reduce to the
linear parameter.

\begin{proposition}\label[proposition]{prop:parity}
Let $T$ be a tetrahedral facet of the mesh whose four realized faces lie
in a geodesic ball of radius less than $\pi/2$. If $C$ meets these faces
transversally, at points interior to the faces, then it meets them in an
even number of points.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The four faces glue along their common edges into a map
$\phi:S^2\to\CP^2$. A geodesic ball of radius less than $\pi/2$ around
$p$ lies in the affine chart $\{x:\langle x,p\rangle\ne0\}\cong\C^2$, which
is contractible, so $\phi$ extends to a map of the $3$-ball. Its image is
a singular $3$-chain $D$ with $\partial D=\phi(S^2)$. Since $C$ is closed,
its intersection number with a boundary vanishes, so
$\#(C\cap\phi(S^2))\equiv0\pmod 2$.
\end{proof}

With chart-flat faces the four faces do not glue, $\phi$ is not defined,
and odd counts occur. In practice the hypothesis on the ball holds for
all cells after the first few levels.

\subsection{Per-cell extraction}

For a leaf $c$, we compute the crossings of $C$ with the $10$ triangular
$2$-faces of $c$, join them into arcs on its $5$ tetrahedral facets, and
assemble the arcs into closed polygons that approximate $C\cap c$.
Crossings are cached by the vertex identifiers of their face, so adjacent
cells see identical crossings.

Why $2$-faces: a $k$-simplex and the real surface $C$ meet, generically,
in a set of dimension $k+2-4$. So $C$ misses the edges and meets each
triangular face in isolated points; it meets each tetrahedral facet in
arcs whose endpoints lie on the four triangles of that facet; and it
meets the cell in a surface piece whose boundary is a closed curve
through the facets. The extraction follows these dimensions upward.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Crossings.} On a face, $F(v(s,t))=0$ is a system of two real
  equations in $(s,t)$; since $F$ is homogeneous, $v$ need not be
  normalized. Newton's method uses the analytic Jacobian of
  $(s,t)\mapsto F(v(s,t))$, and is started from the zero of the linear
  interpolant of $F$ at the aligned vertices and from four fixed points
  of the face. Distinct roots in $[0,1]^2$ are kept, up to two per face.
\item \emph{Arcs.} On each tetrahedral facet, two crossings are joined by
  an arc. With four crossings, they are paired so as to minimize the
  total Fubini--Study length of the two arcs. A single crossing (the
  curve only touches the facet) is ignored, and three or more than four
  make the facet \emph{irregular}.
\item \emph{Polygons.} The arcs form a graph on the crossings of the cell.
  If every node has degree $2$, the graph is a disjoint union of cycles,
  each with $3$ to $5$ nodes, and each cycle is output as one polygon,
  oriented by the sign of the Kähler form $\omega$ on it, that is, by the complex orientation of $C$.
\end{itemize}
A crossing on an edge or at a vertex, up to a tolerance, is identified by
that edge or vertex, so that all cells containing it agree on its
identity. A cell is \emph{bad} when the assembly fails: a node of degree
other than $2$, an irregular facet, or a cycle with fewer than $3$ or
more than $5$ nodes. A planar piece meets a $4$-simplex in a convex
polygon with at most one edge per facet, so longer cycles signal strong
curvature or a near-tangency. With geodesic faces, the remaining failures
occur at near-tangencies (\Cref{sec:exp-badrate}). A bad cell contributes no polygon and leaves a
hole in the output.

\subsection{Continuation}

Starting from one seed cell that meets $C$, a frontier of cells is
processed as follows:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item a cell that meets $C$ and is below the target depth is bisected with
  \texttt{compat\_bisect}, and its new leaves join the frontier;
\item a cell at the target depth is extracted, and its neighbors across
  the five facets, found by \texttt{neighbor\_leaf}, join the frontier.
\end{itemize}
Moving through the mesh thus uses only the combinatorial operations of
\Cref{sec:lpt}. The continuation reaches every cell whose $2$-faces are
crossed by a connected curve. A small closed component of $C\cap c$ that
meets no $2$-face of $c$ would be missed, and a reducible curve needs one
starting cell per component.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Continuation to target depth $D$}
\label{alg:continuation}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $s\gets$ the first seed cell whose extraction has at least one arc
\State $Q\gets(s)$;\ $\mathit{visited}\gets\varnothing$
\While{$Q\ne\varnothing$}
  \State $c\gets\mathrm{pop}(Q)$
  \If{$c$ is not a current leaf \textbf{or} $c\in\mathit{visited}$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \State add $c$ to $\mathit{visited}$; extract $c$
  \If{$c$ has no arc} \textbf{continue} \Comment{the curve does not reach $c$}\EndIf
  \If{$\level(c)<D$ \textbf{or} $c$ is near-tangent (\Cref{sec:tangency})}
    \State $\texttt{compat\_bisect}(c)$; push the new leaves onto $Q$
  \Else
    \For{$i=0,\dots,d$}
      \State \textbf{if} $\texttt{neighbor\_leaf}(c,i)$ finds a leaf $c'\notin\mathit{visited}$ \textbf{then} push $c'$ onto $Q$
    \EndFor
  \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State extract every leaf that meets $C$; output the polygons of the good cells
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\Cref{alg:continuation} summarizes the procedure.

\subsection{Refinement near tangencies}
\label{sec:tangency}

Two optional mechanisms refine further where extraction is likely to
fail:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item a \emph{tangency threshold} on a Cauchy--Riemann transversality
  score computed at each crossing;
\item \emph{repair rounds} that re-bisect bad cells.
\end{itemize}

The \emph{transversality score} of a crossing $x=[v(s,t)]$ on a face is
\[
  \tau(x)=\frac{|\mathrm{Im}(\bar w_1 w_2)|}{|e_1|\,|e_2|\,|\nabla F(v)|^2},\qquad
  w_k=\nabla F(v)\cdot e_k,
\]
where $e_1,e_2$ are $\partial_sv,\partial_tv$ projected orthogonally to $v$,
and $\nabla F=(\partial_XF,\partial_YF,\partial_ZF)$. Since $F$ is
holomorphic and $\nabla F(v)\cdot v=nF(v)=0$, $w_k$ is the derivative of
$F$ along the face in the direction $e_k$, and $\mathrm{Im}(\bar w_1w_2)$ is the Jacobian determinant of
$(\mathrm{Re}\,F,\mathrm{Im}\,F)$ restricted to the face. By
Cauchy--Schwarz, $\tau\in[0,1]$, with $\tau=0$ exactly when the plane of
the face is not transverse to $C$ at $x$. A cell's score is the minimum
over its crossings. With a threshold $t>0$, a cell at the target depth
whose score is below $t$ is bisected further, up to a fixed number of
extra levels (four by default). A repair round bisects every bad leaf
once, within its own budget of extra levels (also four by default), and
extracts every leaf again.

\section{Topological verification and closing}
\label{sec:topology}
% =====================================================================

The output is not certified. Its topology can nevertheless be checked,
directly on the extracted $2$-complex in $\CP^2$, independently of any
projection. Vertices are identified by the mesh vertex, edge or $2$-face
on which the crossing lies. Bad cells leave holes. We split each
\emph{pinched} vertex into one copy per component of its link. In all our
runs these vertices lie on hole boundaries, where a hole touches itself
or two holes meet. If the holes are discs, then
$g=(2c-b-\chi)/2$, where $c$ is the number of components and $b$ the number
of boundary loops. A closing step then turns the complex into a closed
surface:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item it removes every polygon on an edge shared by more than two
  polygons. Each cell passes its own checks, but the cells do not agree
  there; we have seen this only at near-tangencies, where a $2$-face
  carries two close crossings and several cells join them;
\item it keeps the main component;
\item it caps each hole by a cone from a new apex, placed at the loop's
  Fubini--Study barycenter and moved onto $C$ by Newton's method;
\item it checks that the result is a connected closed surface with
  $\chi=2-2g$.
\end{itemize}
These are necessary conditions. A missing handle and a spurious one would
cancel in $\chi$, and $\chi$ says nothing about the embedding. They are
nevertheless a useful sanity check for an uncertified method.

homogeneous representatives, has median below $1.5\times10^{-16}$,
$99$th percentile below $7.7\times10^{-16}$ and maximum below
$7.2\times10^{-14}$. These are the crossings of $C$ with faces of the
mesh, so they lie on the curve to working precision.

Wirtinger's theorem gives a global check on the polygons between them.
We measure the Fubini--Study area of the closed surface, fanning each
polygon into triangles measured in the horizontal tangent space at one
vertex, and compare it with $n\pi$:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
curve & depth & area$/n\pi$, raw & area$/n\pi$, closed \\
\midrule
conic & 14 & $0.99989$ & $1.00003$ \\
elliptic & 14 & $1.00111$ & $1.00114$ \\
elliptic & 18 & $1.00043$ & $1.00046$ \\
Fermat cubic & 14 & $1.00078$ & $1.00142$ \\
Fermat quartic & 17 & $1.00079$ & $1.00128$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The area is within $0.15\%$ of the exact value in every run. In base
mode, the error decreases steadily with depth
(\texttt{area\_sweep.sh}): for the closed surface it falls from
$0.40$--$0.92\%$ at depth $10$ to $0.06$--$0.13\%$ at depth $18$ on the
elliptic curve and the Fermat cubic and quartic, and stays below $0.03\%$
for the conic. The closed surface always slightly overestimates the area. The raw
extraction lies below it by the area of the holes, which can bring it
slightly under $n\pi$; both converge to $n\pi$.

\subsection{Higher degree}
\label{sec:exp-degree}

To see how the method scales with the degree, we added the Fermat
curves of degree $5$ and $6$ and, for $n=3,\dots,6$, a random curve from
the Kostlan ensemble: the coefficient of $X^iY^jZ^k$ is a complex
Gaussian of variance $n!/(i!\,j!\,k!)$. This distribution is invariant
under unitary changes of coordinates, so the curve has no special
position relative to the seed, and the curve is smooth with probability
$1$. At depth $16$ (\texttt{degree\_sweep.sh}):

\midrule
Fermat & 3 & 1  & 54\,272  & $0.044\%$ & $0.035\%$ & $1.00127$ & $27$ \\
       & 4 & 3  & 76\,531  & $0.077\%$ & $0.040\%$ & $1.00193$ & $36$ \\

       & 6 & 10 & 114\,605 & $0.079\%$ & $0.036\%$ & $1.00321$ & $51$ \\
\midrule
Kostlan & 3 & 1  & 51\,736  & $0.070\%$ & $0.026\%$ & $1.00081$ & $29$ \\

``Combined'' is tangency threshold $0.05$ with two repair rounds; the
area is that of the closed surface, the time that of the base mode. Up to
degree $6$ the method does not degrade:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item the number of cells that meet $C$ grows like the area $n\pi$, with
  $17$--$19$ thousand cells per unit of degree, so the cost of a curve is
  proportional to its degree at fixed depth;
\item the bad-cell rate grows only from $0.04$ to $0.10\%$ in base mode,
  and stays at $0.025$--$0.051\%$ with refinement near tangencies;
\item in all $48$ runs (depths $12$, $14$ and $16$, both modes) the closed
  surface is connected and has $\chi=2-2g$, up to genus $10$; $|F|$ at
  the extracted vertices is below $1.8\times10^{-15}$;
\item the closed surface overestimates the area by $0.08$ to $0.34\%$,
  more for higher degree; the raw extraction is within $0.14\%$.
\end{itemize}
Higher degree means more curvature per cell at a given depth, and with
it more near-tangencies. On the Fermat sextic, three runs produced an
edge shared by four polygons: a $2$-face with two crossings $0.035$ apart,
joined by several cells that each pass their own checks. Removing these
polygons before closing (\Cref{sec:topology}) gives the right genus in
all three. The Kostlan curves are slower than the Fermat curves of the
same degree because their polynomials are dense ($28$ terms for $n=6$,
against $3$), and evaluating $F$ dominates the running time.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:exp-cost}

We measured one full incidence structure, VGTL's
\texttt{nmt<4>}~\cite{vgtl}, against the pointerless leaf table of
\Cref{sec:lpt}, on uniform refinement of Gaifullin's seed with no
coordinates and no other data. Both hold exactly the same $108\cdot2^D$
leaves.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{\texttt{nmt<4>}} & & \multicolumn{2}{c}{pointerless} \\
\cmidrule{2-3}\cmidrule{5-6}
$D$ (leaves) & cells stored & bytes/leaf & & table & bytes/leaf \\
\midrule
8 ($27\,648$) & $55\,188$ & 222 & & 0.5 MB & 19.5 \\

\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\texttt{nmt<4>} keeps every cell ever created, so that coarsening remains
possible, together with all faces; this costs $222$ bytes per leaf. The
pointerless table stores one 64-bit code per leaf at load factor $0.41$,
i.e.\ $19.5$ bytes per leaf. That is about $11\times$ less than this full
incidence structure, and about $2\times$ less than the $40$-byte compact
structure estimated in \Cref{sec:lpt}. Neither figure includes per-vertex
data such as coordinates, which both approaches need when geometry is
required.

Continuation from a single seed cell produces exactly the same leaf set
as continuation started from all $108$ seed cells: we compared the two by
an order-independent hash of the leaf codes, for the four curves at
depths $8$, $12$ and $14$, and they agree in all twelve cases. Since the
curve is connected, nothing is lost by starting from one cell. The mesh
is also much smaller than with an earlier criterion, a priority queue
driven by the variation of the gradient, which refines cells that do not
meet the curve as well: by a factor of $1.2$--$1.5$ at depth $8$,
$2.0$--$2.6$ at depth $12$ and $3.1$--$4.3$ at depth $14$.

\subsection{Comparison with an affine chart}
\label{sec:exp-affine}

The most direct alternative is to mesh $\{F(x,y,1)=0\}$ in an affine
chart, as Weigle and Banks do on uniform grids of
$\R^4$~\cite{weiglebanks1996}. We built this baseline from the same code,
changing only the geometry. The seed is Kuhn's triangulation of the box
$[-R,R]^4$ in the coordinates $(\mathrm{Re}\,x,\mathrm{Im}\,x,\mathrm{Re}\,y,
\mathrm{Im}\,y)$: $24$ cells, colour-ordered, with boundary, which the
pointerless mesh accepts once the seed is a parameter. New vertices are
affine midpoints, and every face is flat in the one chart, so the faces
glue exactly here as well. Continuation, extraction and the topological
checks are unchanged. The curve is moved by the same random unitary
transformation that moves Gaifullin's seed in the intrinsic runs; without
it, a curve with real coefficients has its real locus inside a union of
faces of the axis-aligned box, and extraction degenerates.

To compare the two meshes at equal resolution, we refine every cell that
meets $C$ until its Fubini--Study diameter is below $h$. With $h=0.2$:

\small

\toprule

\midrule
elliptic & $\CP^2$ & 1\,023\,146 & 59\,287 & 16 & 7 & $1.000$ & $28$ \\
 & box, $R=2$ & 322\,516 & 19\,814 & 9 & 5 & $0.784$ & $11$ \\
 & box, $R=4$ & 562\,258 & 33\,166 & 9 & 5 & $0.923$ & $20$ \\
 & box, $R=8$ & 810\,518 & 47\,642 & 12 & 7 & $0.981$ & $31$ \\
 & box, $R=16$ & 1\,065\,792 & 62\,365 & 12 & 7 & $0.994$ & $45$ \\
\midrule
Fermat quartic & $\CP^2$ & 1\,396\,928 & 85\,555 & 68 & 25 & $0.999$ & $41$ \\

 & box, $R=4$ & 753\,240 & 46\,011 & 54 & 18 & $0.906$ & $35$ \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Here $b$ counts the boundary loops of the raw extraction: holes left by
bad cells and, in the box, the loops where $C$ leaves it. The runs with
$h=0.3$ (in \texttt{affine.csv}) show the same pattern:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Coverage.} The box misses neighbourhoods of the points of $C$
  at infinity. The missing area falls roughly like $1/R^2$, from $22\%$ at
  $R=2$ to $0.6\%$ at $R=16$ for the elliptic curve, and from $29\%$ to
  $1.3\%$ for the quartic. The intrinsic mesh recovers the whole area,
  within $0.13\%$ at $h=0.2$ and $0.26\%$ at $h=0.3$, the difference being
  the holes left by bad cells.
\item \emph{Cost.} Points far out in the chart are close together in
  $\CP^2$, so a box large enough to cover most of the curve needs many
  levels of refinement near its boundary. At $R=16$ the box already uses
  $4$--$10\%$ more leaves than the intrinsic mesh of all of $\CP^2$ at
  $h=0.2$, and $33$--$45\%$ more at $h=0.3$.
\item \emph{Topology.} The box output is a surface with boundary, whose
  genus can be inferred only when the loops around infinity bound discs,
  and which cannot be closed by capping holes, since some boundary loops
  are not holes. The intrinsic output closes into a surface with
  $\chi=2-2g$.
\end{itemize}
Bad cells are rare in both meshes. In one chart, flat faces glue just as
geodesic faces do in $\CP^2$, which confirms that the gluing of faces, not
the use of charts as such, is what matters for extraction. Covering all
of $\CP^2$ with charts would take three of them, and their meshes would
have to be stitched along the overlaps, where the faces are flat in
different charts and no longer glue: precisely the situation that
geodesic faces avoid.

\subsection{Comparison with \texorpdfstring{$\CP^1\times\CP^1$}{CP1 x CP1}}
\label{sec:exp-s2s2}

The product compactification $\CP^1\times\CP^1\supset\C^2$ is the other
natural setting, and it is also the one of our earlier implementation. It
is not neutral with respect to the curve. The closure of the elliptic curve
$w^2=z^3-z$ in $\CP^1\times\CP^1$ has bidegree $(2,3)$, so if it were smooth
its genus would be $(2-1)(3-1)=2$; since the curve has genus $1$, the
closure is singular. In the coordinates $u=1/w$, $v=1/z$ near
$(\infty,\infty)$ the equation becomes $v^3=u^2(1-v^2)$, a cusp $v^3=u^2$
at the origin. In $\CP^2$ the closure $X^2Z=Y^3-YZ^2$ is smooth, and the
method needs no special care at infinity.

To measure what the cusp costs, we ran the same pipeline on
$\CP^1\times\CP^1$, changing only the seed and the geometry
(\texttt{riemann\_s2s2\_glpt}). The seed is the product of two octahedra,
triangulated by staircases: $36$ vertices and $384$ cells. It is
balanced: colouring each octahedron by its three antipodal pairs and the
vertex $(a,b)$ by $\col(a)+\col(b)$ gives every staircase the colours
$0,\dots,4$ in order. New vertices are Fubini--Study midpoints in each
factor, faces are geodesic cones built factor by factor as in
\Cref{sec:faces}, and $F$ is evaluated bihomogeneously, with no chart.
With the Fubini--Study metric on each factor, a curve of bidegree
$(n_w,n_z)$ has area $\pi(n_w+n_z)$, which plays the role of Wirtinger's
theorem. We compare the elliptic curve with the parabola $w^2=z$, whose
closure $v=u^2$ is smooth at the corner, in base mode; ``near'' means a
cell with a vertex within $0.1$ of $(\infty,\infty)$ in the product
metric:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrc@{}}
\toprule
curve & depth & bad near / near & bad elsewhere & area$/\pi(n_w{+}n_z)$ & closed $\chi$ \\
\midrule
elliptic & 12 & 19 / 111 & $0.092\%$ & $1.00044$ & $0$ \\
         & 14 & 23 / 300 & $0.058\%$ & $1.00024$ & $0$ \\
         & 16 & 30 / 576 & $0.042\%$ & $1.00012$ & $0$ \\
\midrule
parabola & 12 & 0 / 42  & $0.065\%$ & $0.99947$ & $2$ \\
         & 14 & 0 / 124 & $0.021\%$ & $0.99971$ & $2$ \\
         & 16 & 0 / 232 & $0.014\%$ & $0.99987$ & $2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Away from the corner both curves behave as in $\CP^2$. At the corner the
parabola has no failure at all, while $5$ to $18\%$ of the elliptic
curve's cells there fail, at every depth and in five further rotations of
the seed at depth $14$. Their number grows slowly with depth while the
area they cover shrinks, as expected near a singular point where the
curvature of $C$ is unbounded.

The cusp also affects the cells that do not fail. Some of their polygons
cut across the cusp instead of following the curve, and they glue with
the wrong orientation: without further care the extracted complex is not
orientable, and closing it gives $\chi=-1$. A holomorphic curve carries a
canonical orientation, and the Kähler form $\omega$ of the ambient space
restricts to its area form, so a polygon close to the curve has
$|\omega|/\mathrm{area}$ close to $1$, the value attained exactly on
complex lines. Treating as bad every cell with a polygon below $0.9$
removes $1$ to $18$ cells of the elliptic curve per run, mostly near the
corner, and at most one of the parabola; the closed surface is then an
orientable torus in all nine runs of \texttt{s2s2\_sweep.sh}. The test
applies equally in $\CP^2$, where we have not needed it. Our earlier $\CP^1\times\CP^1$ implementation, with
chart-flat faces and a switch to the chart $(1/w,1/z)$ near the corner,
failed at the corner for both curves; with geodesic cones that failure
disappears, which isolates the cost of the cusp. In $\CP^2$ the closure is
smooth and neither the cusp nor the corner arises.

\section{Visualization}
\label{sec:viz}
% =====================================================================

The triangulation lives in $\CP^2$; to draw it, we map it to $\R^3$.
We use two maps.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Flat charts.} A point of the chart $U_k$ with coordinates
  $(a,b)$ is drawn at $(\mathrm{Re}\,a,\mathrm{Im}\,a,\mathrm{Re}\,b)$,
  i.e.\ as the graph of $\mathrm{Re}\,b$ over the $a$-plane, clipped to a
  box. The chart is chosen per polygon, as the best-conditioned chart
  of its vertices, or fixed for the whole picture. A per-polygon choice
  keeps every polygon bounded, but adjacent polygons drawn in different
  charts do not match: the picture is a union of pieces of three graphs.
  A fixed chart gives one consistent graph, but loses the curve near the
  line at infinity of that chart. \Cref{fig:riemann}(a) uses the fixed
  chart $Z=1$.
\item \emph{Dutter's map}~\cite{dutter2026}, after
  Kranich~\cite{kranich2016}, maps all of $\CP^2$ continuously into a
  bounded region of $\R^3$, with no chart. It shows the whole closed
  surface (\Cref{fig:riemann}(b)), at the cost of self-intersections in
  the image.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
% =====================================================================

We triangulate smooth plane curves intrinsically in $\CP^2$ by adaptive
Maubach bisection of a balanced seed, continuation through a pointerless
mesh, and polygons joining the points where the curve meets the $2$-faces. Working in $\CP^2$ removes charts,
cuts and the special role of infinity. Balance of the seed is what makes
both bisection and the pointerless neighbor procedure work on a closed
manifold, and the same code runs unchanged on $\CP^1\times\CP^1$ once its
seed and geometry are swapped.

The method has clear limits.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item It is not certified. The bad-cell rate decreases with depth but does
  not vanish, and the topology is checked only a posteriori, through
  necessary conditions.
\item It assumes a smooth curve. At the cusp that $\CP^1\times\CP^1$
  creates on the elliptic curve, $5$ to $18\%$ of the nearby cells fail at
  every depth, and some of the others glue with the wrong orientation. The
  Kähler test removes the latter, but a correct extraction at a singular
  point needs a different method.
\item Continuation can miss a small component of $C\cap c$ that meets no
  $2$-face of $c$. A reducible curve needs one starting cell per
  component.
\end{itemize}
The most natural next step is certification. A per-cell test in the
spirit of Plantinga and Vegter, adapted to codimension two, can use a
single complex condition: that an enclosure of
$\langle\nabla F(x),\nabla F(x_0)\rangle$ over the cell, with $x_0$ its
center, excludes $0$. With the implicit function theorem and the
Noshiro--Warschawski theorem, this makes $C\cap c$ the graph of a
holomorphic function over a complex line. On geodesic faces $F$ is no longer a
polynomial in the face coordinates, so the enclosures must come from
interval arithmetic rather than Bernstein coefficients. Other open
directions are curves of degree well beyond $6$, the Kähler test as a general
consistency check in $\CP^2$, and other complex surfaces that admit small
balanced seeds.
