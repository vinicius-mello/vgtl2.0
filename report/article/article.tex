\begin{abstract}
We give a self-contained combinatorial account of Maubach's newest-vertex
bisection as a \emph{stellar subdivision scheme}: an invariant on ordered
simplicial meshes that holds at every level of the hierarchy, together
with a subdivision algorithm that provably preserves it. We then turn to
the initial mesh. Strong compatibility of a seed is equivalent to the
seed being balanced, i.e.\ properly $(d{+}1)$-colorable, a fact implicit
in Traxler's work and in recent coloring-based initializations. We use
it to connect bisection with the classes of triangulations of closed
manifolds studied by Dynnikov, Novikov and Gaifullin. In particular,
Gaifullin's 15-vertex triangulation of $\CP^2$ is a strongly compatible
seed with the fewest possible vertices. Balance is also what makes Atalay
and Mount's pointerless LPT codes portable beyond the cube. On a balanced
seed, a precomputed table plus one integer comparison decides whether a
facet lies on the seed boundary, and the neighbor across it carries the
same code in the adjacent seed cell. This yields a conforming, graded,
pointerless Maubach bisection over any balanced seed, with no floating
point. As an example, we triangulate smooth plane curves intrinsically
inside $\CP^2$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

Newest-vertex bisection in the form given by Maubach~\cite{maubach1995}
and Traxler~\cite{traxler1997} is the standard way to refine simplicial
meshes locally in arbitrary dimension $d$. Its refinement decisions depend
only on combinatorial data: a vertex order in each cell and the cell's
level in the hierarchy. It keeps meshes conforming, produces finitely
many similarity classes when new vertices are placed at
midpoints~\cite{maubach1995,amp2000}, and satisfies the closure estimates
behind optimal adaptive finite elements~\cite{bdd2004,stevenson2008}. Its
well-known weakness is the initial mesh. The vertex orders of the
initial cells must be \emph{compatible}, and for $d\ge3$ not every mesh
admits compatible orders. A substantial literature addresses how to
repair this~\cite{kossaczky1994,stevenson2008,agk2018,belda2023,dgs2025}.

This paper studies Maubach bisection from a purely combinatorial
standpoint, with two goals in mind: to state and prove its correctness
through local stellar operations alone, and to run it pointerlessly on
seeds more general than the Kuhn cube. The original motivation was
geometric: to triangulate curves inside $\CP^2$, whose natural
triangulations are neither cubes nor domains in $\R^d$.

\paragraph{Stellar subdivision schemes (\Cref{sec:stellar,sec:maubach}).}
Following the author's thesis~\cite{mello2006}, a \emph{subdivision
scheme} is an invariant on ordered simplicial meshes together with an
algorithm that propagates ordered stellar subdivisions until a requested
cell can be split without violating the invariant. We give complete
proofs for two schemes, Mitchell's (split a facet) and Maubach's (split
an edge), which share the same three-lemma structure. For Maubach's
scheme, the invariant is \emph{hereditary}: it holds at every level, not
only on the initial mesh as in the conditions of Traxler and Stevenson.
We prove one direction of its relation to Stevenson's matching condition.

\paragraph{Balanced seeds (\Cref{sec:initial}).}
On a conforming mesh, strong compatibility (every shared facet sits at the
same local index on both sides) is equivalent to the mesh being
\emph{balanced}. This equivalence is implicit in Traxler's
work~\cite{traxler1997} and explicit, in the language of vertex colorings,
in the recent initialization of Diening, Gehring and Storn~\cite{dgs2025};
we include a short proof. Our contribution is to connect it with the
classes of triangulations of closed manifolds studied by Dynnikov and
Novikov and by Gaifullin~\cite{gaifullin2009}, which the bisection
literature does not consider. For simply connected manifolds these
classes coincide, so a seed is strongly compatible if and only if every
codimension-2 face has even degree. Gaifullin proved that his 15-vertex
triangulation of $\CP^2$ is vertex-minimal among balanced triangulations
of $\CP^2$. It follows that it is a strongly compatible seed of $\CP^2$ with
the fewest possible vertices. Kühnel's 9-vertex $\CP^2_9$ is not
balanced.

\paragraph{Pointerless codes on balanced seeds (\Cref{sec:lpt}).}
Atalay and Mount's LPT codes~\cite{atalaymount2007} represent a Maubach
hierarchy over a Kuhn-triangulated cube with no pointers. The geometry
and the neighbors of a cell are computed from a small integer code. We
show that balance is all that is needed to use these codes over an
arbitrary seed:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item whether a facet lies on the seed boundary is decided by a table
  indexed by the code's level, signed permutation and facet, followed by
  one comparison of orthant coordinates (\Cref{prop:boundary-test});
\item the neighbor across a seed-boundary facet is the same code in the
  adjacent seed cell (\Cref{prop:crossing}).
\end{itemize}
Every neighbor query is therefore resolved combinatorially. We verify the
resulting procedure exactly on five million queries, and compare its
memory use with that of explicit incidence structures.

\paragraph{Example (\Cref{sec:example}).}
We triangulate smooth plane curves directly inside $\CP^2$. The seed is
Gaifullin's triangulation, refinement is pointerless Maubach bisection
along Fubini--Study geodesics, and the curve is followed by a
continuation driven only by combinatorial neighbor queries.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item Complete, self-contained proofs of correctness for the Mitchell and
  Maubach stellar subdivision schemes, with a hereditary invariant, and
  one direction of its relation to Stevenson's matching condition.
\item The connection between strongly compatible seeds and the classes of
  balanced, chess-colorable and even triangulations of closed manifolds.
  As a consequence, Gaifullin's triangulation is a vertex-minimal strongly
  compatible seed of $\CP^2$.
\item An extension of LPT codes to arbitrary balanced seeds with a fully
  combinatorial neighbor procedure, an exact verification, and a
  memory comparison with explicit incidence structures.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Bisection and initial meshes.}
Longest-edge bisection goes back to Rivara~\cite{rivara1984,rivara1991}.
Newest-vertex bisection was introduced in 2D by
Mitchell~\cite{mitchell1988,mitchell1991}, extended to 3D by
Bänsch~\cite{bansch1991} and Kossaczký~\cite{kossaczky1994}, and to
arbitrary dimension by Maubach~\cite{maubach1995} and
Traxler~\cite{traxler1997}. Arnold, Mukherjee and Pouly~\cite{amp2000}
gave a marked variant for tetrahedra that accepts any initial mesh.
Binev, Dahmen and DeVore~\cite{bdd2004} and Stevenson~\cite{stevenson2008}
proved the closure estimates behind the optimality of adaptive finite
elements. All of these methods constrain the initial mesh:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item Traxler's \emph{reflected} condition requires the ordered vertex
  lists of neighbors to differ in exactly one position; on a simply
  connected domain it holds iff every interior $(d{-}2)$-face has even
  degree~\cite{traxler1997}.
\item Stevenson's weaker \emph{matching-neighbour} condition is necessary
  and sufficient for all uniform refinements to be conforming. Whether
  every 3D mesh admits it is open; following Kossaczký, Stevenson
  pre-refines every simplex into $\frac12(d{+}1)!$
  pieces~\cite{stevenson2008}.
\item Alkämper, Gaspoz and Klöfkorn~\cite{agk2018} give an $O(n)$
  renumbering that guarantees termination.
\item Belda-Ferrín et al.~\cite{belda2018,belda2022,belda2023} perform $d$
  marked-bisection stages before switching to Maubach.
\item Diening, Gehring and Storn~\cite{dgs2025} initialize Maubach's
  routine from a proper $(N{+}1)$-coloring of the vertices, $N\ge d$,
  computed greedily in linear time, and prove shape regularity and the
  closure estimate. For $N=d$ their condition is Traxler's.
\end{itemize}
All of the above concern triangulations of domains in $\R^d$. We add the
hereditary formulation of the invariant, and seeds that triangulate
closed manifolds.

\paragraph{Balanced and chess-colorable triangulations.}
A $d$-complex is \emph{balanced} if its graph is $(d{+}1)$-colorable.
Joswig~\cite{joswig2002} characterized balanced complexes through their
group of projectivities. Dynnikov and Novikov studied triangulations with
regular vertex colorings, chess colorings of top simplices, and even
codimension-2 degrees in connection with discrete connections and
discrete complex analysis; Gaifullin~\cite[\S2]{gaifullin2009} summarizes
the relations between these classes and constructs a vertex-minimal
balanced triangulation of $\CP^2$. Izmestiev, Klee and
Novik~\cite{ikn2017} introduced cross-flips, the balanced analogue of
bistellar moves, and Venturello~\cite{venturello2019} found small
balanced triangulations of several manifolds. Stellar operations go back
to Alexander and Newman~\cite{lickorish1999}; in geometry processing,
Velho's stellar subdivision grammars~\cite{velho2003} and
$4$--$8$ meshes~\cite{velhozorin2001} describe adaptive surface refinement
by stellar operations, and $4$--$8$ refinement is newest-vertex bisection
in two dimensions.

\paragraph{Pointerless hierarchical meshes.}
Several representations encode a simplex of a regular bisection hierarchy
by a label instead of pointers: Hebert~\cite{hebert1994}; Evans,
Kirkpatrick and Townsend~\cite{ekt2001}; Lee, De Floriani and
Samet~\cite{ldfs2001}; Maubach's own neighbor location~\cite{maubach1996};
and Atalay and Mount's LPT codes~\cite{atalaymount2007} in arbitrary
dimension, which we previously used for adaptive cellular structures in
3D printing~\cite{sa2015adaptive}. Weiss and De Floriani use the star of
the bisection edge (a \emph{diamond}) as primitive~\cite{weissdefloriani2011};
Dupuy's concurrent binary trees implement longest-edge bisection on the
GPU~\cite{dupuy2020}; Boissonnat, Kachanovich and Wintraecken encode
Freudenthal--Kuhn simplices compactly, without adaptivity~\cite{bkw2023}.
Architecturally closest to our construction are the forests of
p4est~\cite{p4est2011} and t8code~\cite{bursteddeholke2016}: an arbitrary
coarse mesh refined tree by tree, with a per-tree linear code and an
inter-tree connectivity table. Those forests are non-conforming (hanging
nodes, red refinement, $d\le3$); ours is a conforming, graded bisection
forest in arbitrary dimension.

\paragraph{Implicit curves and surfaces.}
The example of \Cref{sec:example} relates to piecewise-linear
approximation of implicit manifolds over Freudenthal--Kuhn
triangulations~\cite{allgowerschmidt1985,allgowergeorg2003}, to its
certified, uniform-grid variant by cell-to-cell
tracing~\cite{bkw2023,boissonnatwintraecken2022}, to the extraction of
zero sets of functions $\C^2\to\C$ on uniform grids of
$\R^4$~\cite{weiglebanks1996}, and to numerical and visual approaches to
compact Riemann surfaces through branched coverings and charts~\cite{deconinckvanhoeij2001,bobenkoklein2013,nieser2010,kranich2016}.

\section{Ordered meshes and stellar subdivision schemes}
\label{sec:stellar}
% =====================================================================

Adaptive refinement has a geometric side (where new vertices go, how cell
shapes evolve) and a combinatorial side (which simplex to split next, and
which additional splits keep the mesh conforming). Newest-vertex
bisection is attractive precisely because its decisions can be made from
combinatorial data alone, which makes it usable on meshes whose geometry
is unusual, such as a triangulation of $\CP^2$ realized with
Fubini--Study geodesics. This section isolates that combinatorial layer.
A refinement \emph{scheme} is an invariant on ordered meshes together
with an algorithm that preserves it; correctness then reduces to checking
how one stellar operation affects pairs of adjacent cells. We illustrate
the framework on Mitchell's scheme, whose proof has the same structure as
Maubach's but fewer cases.

Throughout, $d$ denotes the dimension of the mesh.

\subsection{Ordered simplicial complexes and conforming meshes}

\begin{definition}
An \emph{ordered simplicial complex} $K$ on a finite vertex set $V$ is an
abstract simplicial complex with a partial order on $V$ that is total on
the vertices of every simplex. We write
$\sigma=\langle v_0,\dots,v_k\rangle$ with $v_0<\dots<v_k$ and
$\sigma[p]=v_p$. The \emph{face operator} $\partial_i$ deletes the $i$-th
vertex, and $\sigma[p_0,\dots,p_q]:=\langle v_{p_0},\dots,v_{p_q}\rangle$
for $p_0<\dots<p_q$.
\end{definition}

Equalities such as $\partial_i\sigma=\partial_j\omega$ are equalities of
\emph{ordered} simplices; this will matter in \Cref{rem:index-only}.
The $d$-simplices of a $d$-complex are its \emph{cells} and its
$(d{-}1)$-simplices its \emph{facets}. Two cells are \emph{adjacent} if
they share a facet. $K$ is a \emph{pseudomanifold} if it is pure and
every facet lies in at most two cells. It is \emph{strongly connected}
if any two cells are joined by a chain of adjacent cells.

\begin{definition}[\cite{mello2006}]
A strongly connected pseudomanifold is a \emph{conforming mesh} if the
star $\st(v,K)$ of every vertex is strongly connected.
\end{definition}

Every combinatorial manifold of dimension $d\ge2$ is a conforming mesh.
In a conforming mesh the star of every simplex $\epsilon$ is strongly
connected. Moreover, two cells of $\st(\epsilon,K)$ that share a facet
share a facet containing $\epsilon$, since both contain $\epsilon$. Hence
$\st(\epsilon,K)$ can be traversed by crossing only facets that contain
$\epsilon$.

\subsection{Ordered stellar subdivisions}

Let $\delta\in K$ and let $v$ be a new vertex. The \emph{stellar
subdivision} $K(\delta,v)$ replaces $\st(\delta,K)$ by the cone from $v$
over $\partial\delta\star\link(\delta,K)$. It is an \emph{ordered stellar
subdivision} $K(\delta,v)_l$ if the new vertex is placed at position $l$
in every new cell. A cell $\sigma\supseteq\delta$ with
$\delta=\sigma[p_0,\dots,p_k]$ is replaced by $k+1$ \emph{children}
$c_0\sigma,\dots,c_k\sigma$, one for each facet of $\delta$; the child
$c_q\sigma$ is obtained from $\sigma$ by deleting $\sigma[p_{k-q}]$ and
inserting $v$ at position $l$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:children}
  \partial_l\,c_q\sigma=\partial_{p_{k-q}}\sigma .
\end{equation}
A sequence of ordered stellar subdivisions of an initial mesh $K_0$
induces a hierarchy: $\level(\sigma)=0$ for $\sigma\in K_0$ and
$\level(c_q\sigma)=\level(\sigma)+1$. A cell of positive level has a
unique parent, and a unique \emph{weld vertex} $\weld(\sigma)$: the
vertex inserted by the subdivision that created it.

\subsection{Subdivision schemes}

\begin{definition}[\cite{mello2006}]
A \emph{stellar subdivision scheme} is a pair $(\mathcal I,\mathrm{Subdivide})$
where $\mathcal I$ is a set of invariants on conforming meshes, stated in
terms of vertex order, adjacency and level only, and
$\mathrm{Subdivide}(K,\sigma)$ applies a finite sequence of ordered stellar
subdivisions to $K$, the last of which subdivides a face of $\sigma$ (the
\emph{split face} of $\sigma$), such that every intermediate mesh
satisfies $\mathcal I$.
\end{definition}

A basic invariant bounds the level jump across facets:
\begin{equation}\tag{OneLevel}
  \partial_i\sigma=\partial_j\omega\;\Longrightarrow\;|\level(\sigma)-\level(\omega)|\le1 .
\end{equation}
All invariants below are conditions on \emph{pairs of adjacent cells}.
To show that a stellar subdivision preserves them, it therefore suffices
to check the adjacent pairs involving at least one new cell.

We say that $\mathrm{Subdivide}$ is \emph{consistent} if every stellar
subdivision $(\delta,v)$ it performs splits cells that all have split face
$\delta$; \emph{alternating} if $\weld(\sigma)$ never belongs to the split
face of $\sigma$; and \emph{level-homogeneous} if every stellar
subdivision it performs splits cells of a single level. (The thesis calls
the last property ``balanced''; we rename it to avoid a clash with
balanced complexes.)

\subsection{Mitchell's scheme}
\label{sec:mitchell}

Mitchell's scheme splits the facet opposite the first vertex:
$\msplit(\sigma)=\partial_0\sigma$, with the new vertex inserted at
position $0$. For $\omega=\langle x,v_1,\dots,v_d\rangle$ the children of
$(\partial_0\omega,v)_0$ are, by \eqref{eq:children},
\[
  c_q\omega=\langle v,x,v_1,\dots,\widehat{v_{d-q}},\dots,v_d\rangle,\qquad q=0,\dots,d-1 .
\]

\begin{definition}[Mitchell invariants]
$K$ satisfies the \emph{Mitchell invariants} if it satisfies (OneLevel)
and, for adjacent cells with $\partial_i\sigma=\partial_j\omega$,
\begin{align*}
  \level(\sigma)=\level(\omega)&\;\Longrightarrow\; i=0=j\ \text{ or }\ i\ne0\ne j, \tag{MitchellA}\\
  \level(\sigma)=\level(\omega)+1&\;\Longrightarrow\; i=0\ne j. \tag{MitchellB}
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

Every mesh satisfies these invariants after the cone $(\sigma,v_\sigma)_0$
is applied to each of its cells: all new cells have level $0$, and two
adjacent new cells share a facet that avoids both cone vertices, so
$i\ne0\ne j$.

\begin{lemma}[direction]\label[lemma]{lem:m-direction}
Under the Mitchell invariants, if $\partial_i\sigma=\partial_j\omega$ then
$\level(\omega)\le\level(\sigma)$ when $i=0$, and
$\level(\omega)\ge\level(\sigma)$ when $i\ne0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $\level(\omega)>\level(\sigma)$, then $\level(\omega)=\level(\sigma)+1$
by (OneLevel), and (MitchellB) applied to the pair $(\omega,\sigma)$ gives
$j=0\ne i$. If $\level(\omega)<\level(\sigma)$, (MitchellB) gives
$i=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[children]\label[lemma]{lem:m-children}
Any two children of $(\partial_0\omega,v)_0$ are adjacent, and their
common facet is obtained from each of them by deleting a vertex at a
position $\ge2$. In particular every such pair satisfies (MitchellA).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The children form the cone $v\star\partial\delta\star x$ over the
boundary of the facet $\delta=\partial_0\omega$. The children $c_q\omega$
and $c_r\omega$ omit two different vertices $v_a,v_b$ of $\delta$ and share
the remaining $d$ vertices, which form a facet of both. In $c_q\omega$
this facet is obtained by deleting $v_b$, which sits after $v$ and $x$,
hence at a position $\ge2$; symmetrically in $c_r\omega$. (The thesis
lists only the pairs omitting consecutive vertices, which are all the
pairs when $d\le3$.)
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[two cells]\label[lemma]{lem:m-twocells}
Let $\sigma,\omega$ be adjacent, $\partial_i\sigma=\partial_j\omega$, with
$0\le\level(\sigma)-\level(\omega)\le1$, satisfying the Mitchell
invariants. Subdivide $\omega$ by $(\partial_0\omega,v)_0$, and also
$\sigma$ if $\partial_0\sigma=\partial_0\omega$. Then all adjacent pairs of
new cells, and of new cells with $\sigma$, satisfy the invariants.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By (MitchellA) and (MitchellB), exactly three cases occur.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),itemsep=2pt]
\item $\level(\sigma)=\level(\omega)$, $i=j=0$. Both cells are split
  across their common facet. Pairs of children of the same cell satisfy
  (MitchellA) by \Cref{lem:m-children}. Since
  $\partial_0\sigma=\partial_0\omega$ as ordered simplices, $c_q\sigma$ and
  $c_q\omega$ differ only in position $1$, so
  $\partial_1c_q\sigma=\partial_1c_q\omega$: (MitchellA). A child of
  $\sigma$ and a child of $\omega$ with different indices share only
  $d-1$ vertices.
\item $\level(\sigma)=\level(\omega)$, $i\ne0\ne j$. Only $\omega$ is split.
  The facet $\partial_j\omega$ contains $x=\omega[0]$ and omits
  $v_j=\omega[j]$, so it is $\partial_0c_{d-j}\omega$. The pair
  $(c_{d-j}\omega,\sigma)$ has levels $\level(\sigma)+1,\level(\sigma)$ and
  indices $0\ne i$: (MitchellB).
\item $\level(\sigma)=\level(\omega)+1$, $i=0\ne j$. As in (2),
  $\partial_0\sigma=\partial_j\omega=\partial_0c_{d-j}\omega$, now at equal
  levels with indices $0,0$: (MitchellA).
\end{enumerate}
In all cases the level difference is at most $1$, so (OneLevel) holds.
\end{proof}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{$\textsc{MitchellSubdivide}(K,\sigma)$}
\label{alg:mitchell}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\omega\gets$ the cell adjacent to $\sigma$ across $\partial_0\sigma$ (if any)
\If{$\omega$ exists and $\level(\omega)<\level(\sigma)$}
  \State $\textsc{MitchellSubdivide}(K,\omega)$;\ $\omega\gets$ the cell adjacent to $\sigma$ across $\partial_0\sigma$
\EndIf
\State apply $(\partial_0\sigma,v)_0$ to $\sigma$ and $\omega$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label[theorem]{thm:mitchell}
Let $K$ satisfy the Mitchell invariants. For every cell $\sigma$,
\Cref{alg:mitchell} terminates, only splits cells of level at most
$\level(\sigma)$, and produces a mesh satisfying the Mitchell invariants.
It is consistent, alternating and level-homogeneous.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By induction on $\level(\sigma)$. By \Cref{lem:m-direction} with $i=0$,
the neighbor $\omega$ across $\partial_0\sigma$ has
$\level(\omega)\le\level(\sigma)$. If $\level(\omega)<\level(\sigma)$, the
recursive call has a smaller argument level; by the induction hypothesis
it terminates, splits only cells of level at most $\level(\omega)$ (so not
$\sigma$), and preserves the invariants. By (MitchellB),
$\partial_0\sigma=\partial_j\omega$ with $j\ne0$, so after $\omega$ is
split this facet belongs to the child $c_{d-j}\omega$, of level
$\level(\sigma)$, as its facet $\partial_0$ (case (3) of
\Cref{lem:m-twocells}). In either case, when line 5 is reached, $\sigma$
and the cell $\omega$ across $\partial_0\sigma$ have the same level and
$\partial_0\sigma=\partial_0\omega$. These two cells are the whole star of
the facet $\partial_0\sigma$, both have it as split face (consistency),
and both have the same level (level homogeneity). \Cref{lem:m-twocells},
applied to every adjacent pair involving a new cell, shows that the
invariants are preserved. Finally, the weld vertex $v$ of a child sits at
position $0$ and $\msplit=\partial_0$ omits position $0$, so the scheme is
alternating.
\end{proof}

\section{Maubach's scheme}
\label{sec:maubach}
% =====================================================================

In dimension $2$ a facet is an edge, and Mitchell's scheme is exactly
newest-vertex bisection. In higher dimensions a facet split is no longer
a bisection. Maubach's scheme splits an edge; on Kuhn's triangulation of
the cube it reproduces longest-edge bisection with finitely many
similarity classes~\cite{maubach1995}. This section describes it as a
stellar subdivision scheme: which invariant on ordered meshes guarantees
that the edge chosen by one cell is also the edge chosen by every
neighbor that contains it? The resulting proof of conformity refers only
to vertex orders and levels, and applies to any mesh that satisfies the
invariant, whatever its geometry.

For a cell $\sigma$ of a $d$-mesh set
\[
  \type(\sigma):=d-(\level(\sigma)\bmod d)\in\{1,\dots,d\},\qquad
  \msplit(\sigma):=\sigma[0,\type(\sigma)],
\]
and split $\msplit(\sigma)$ with the new vertex at position
$l=\type(\sigma)$. By \eqref{eq:children}, the children of
$\sigma=\langle v_0,\dots,v_d\rangle$ are
\[
  c_0\sigma=\langle v_0,\dots,v_{l-1},v,v_{l+1},\dots,v_d\rangle,\qquad
  c_1\sigma=\langle v_1,\dots,v_l,v,v_{l+1},\dots,v_d\rangle,
\]
which is exactly the rule of~\cite{maubach1995} and of~\cite[Alg.~1]{dgs2025}.
Since $\type\ne0$, the facet $\partial_p\sigma$ contains $\msplit(\sigma)$
iff $p\notin\{0,\type(\sigma)\}$.

\begin{definition}[Maubach invariants]\label[definition]{def:maubach}
$K$ satisfies the \emph{Maubach invariants} if it satisfies (OneLevel)
and, for adjacent cells with $\partial_i\sigma=\partial_j\omega$,
\begin{align*}
  \level(\sigma)=\level(\omega)&\;\Longrightarrow\; i=j\ \text{ or }\ \{i,j\}=\{0,\type(\omega)\},\tag{MaubachA}\\
  \level(\sigma)=\level(\omega)+1&\;\Longrightarrow\; i=\type(\omega)\ \text{ and }\ j\in\{0,\type(\omega)\}.\tag{MaubachB}
\end{align*}
$K$ is \emph{strongly compatible} if all its cells have level $0$ and
$\partial_i\sigma=\partial_j\omega$ implies $i=j$; such a mesh satisfies
the Maubach invariants.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[direction]\label[lemma]{lem:direction}
Under the Maubach invariants, if $\partial_i\sigma=\partial_j\omega$ then
$\level(\omega)\le\level(\sigma)$ when $i\notin\{0,\type(\sigma)\}$, and
$\level(\omega)\ge\level(\sigma)$ when $i\in\{0,\type(\sigma)\}$.
Moreover, if $\level(\omega)=\level(\sigma)$ and
$i\notin\{0,\type(\sigma)\}$, then $i=j$ and $\msplit(\omega)=\msplit(\sigma)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $\level(\omega)>\level(\sigma)$, then (OneLevel) and (MaubachB) for the
pair $(\omega,\sigma)$ give $i\in\{0,\type(\sigma)\}$. If
$\level(\omega)<\level(\sigma)$, (MaubachB) gives $i=\type(\omega)$, and
$\type(\omega)\notin\{0,\type(\sigma)\}$ because consecutive levels have
different types. If the levels are equal and $i\notin\{0,\type(\sigma)\}$,
then $\{i,j\}\ne\{0,\type\}$, so (MaubachA) gives $i=j$; the two cells
then differ only at position $i$, which is outside $\{0,\type\}$, so their
split edges coincide.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[children]\label[lemma]{lem:children}
For a cell $\omega$ and $l=\type(\omega)$, the children satisfy
$\partial_0c_0\omega=\partial_{l-1}c_1\omega$, and this pair satisfies
(MaubachA).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both sides equal $\langle v_1,\dots,v_{l-1},v,v_{l+1},\dots,v_d\rangle$. The
children have equal level and type $l'=\type(c_q\omega)$, which is
$l-1$ if $l>1$ and $d$ if $l=1$. If $l>1$ the indices are $\{0,l-1\}=\{0,l'\}$;
if $l=1$ both indices are $0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[two cells]\label[lemma]{lem:twocells}
Let $\sigma,\omega$ be adjacent, $\partial_i\sigma=\partial_j\omega$, with
$0\le\level(\sigma)-\level(\omega)\le1$, satisfying the Maubach
invariants, and let $l=\type(\omega)$. Split $\omega$ at
$\msplit(\omega)$, and also $\sigma$ if it contains $\msplit(\omega)$. Then
every adjacent pair formed by a child of $\omega$ and $\sigma$ or a child
of $\sigma$ satisfies the Maubach invariants.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We list all cases allowed by (MaubachA) and (MaubachB), writing
$\omega=\langle v_0,\dots,v_d\rangle$. In each case we exhibit the facet
identity. When the two cells have different levels, the coarser one is
$\sigma$, of type $l$, and we check (MaubachB): the finer index must be
$l$ and the coarser index must lie in $\{0,l\}$.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}clll@{}}
\toprule
case & levels, indices & new identity & invariant \\
\midrule
1 & equal, $i=j=0$ & $\partial_0\sigma=\partial_l c_1\omega$ & (B): finer $l$, coarser $0$ \\
2 & equal, $i=j=l$ & $\partial_l\sigma=\partial_l c_0\omega$ & (B): finer $l$, coarser $l$ \\
3 & equal, $i=j=k\notin\{0,l\}$ & $\partial_k c_0\sigma=\partial_k c_0\omega$, $\partial_{k'}c_1\sigma=\partial_{k'}c_1\omega$ & (A): $i=j$ \\
4 & equal, $i=0$, $j=l$ & $\partial_0\sigma=\partial_l c_0\omega$ & (B): finer $l$, coarser $0$ \\
5 & equal, $i=l$, $j=0$ & $\partial_l\sigma=\partial_l c_1\omega$ & (B): finer $l$, coarser $l$ \\
6 & $+1$, $i=l$, $j=0$ & $\partial_l\sigma=\partial_l c_1\omega$ & (A): $i=j$ \\
7 & $+1$, $i=l$, $j=l$ & $\partial_l\sigma=\partial_l c_0\omega$ & (A): $i=j$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In case 3, $k'=k-1$ if $k<l$ and $k'=k$ if $k>l$.

\emph{Completeness.} At equal levels, if $j\notin\{0,l\}$ then the
common facet contains $\msplit(\omega)$, so $\sigma$ is split as well and
$i=j$ by \Cref{lem:direction} (case 3). Otherwise $j\in\{0,l\}$, and
(MaubachA) leaves cases 1, 2, 4 and 5. At levels differing by one,
(MaubachB) for $(\sigma,\omega)$ gives $i=l$, $j\in\{0,l\}$ (cases 6
and 7).

\emph{Identities.} $\partial_0\omega=\langle v_1,\dots,v_d\rangle$ is
$c_1\omega$ with $v$ (at position $l$) deleted, and
$\partial_l\omega=\langle v_0,\dots,\widehat{v_l},\dots,v_d\rangle$ is
$c_0\omega$ with $v$ deleted. This gives cases 1, 2 and 4--7.

In case 3, $\sigma$ and $\omega$ differ only at position $k$ and have the
same split edge. Their children $c_0\sigma$, $c_0\omega$ still differ only
at position $k$. Their children $c_1\sigma$, $c_1\omega$ differ only at
position $k-1$ if $k<l$ (entries $1,\dots,l$ shift down by one) or at
position $k$ if $k>l$. A child of $\sigma$ and a child of $\omega$ with
different indices share at most $d-1$ vertices, so these are the only new
adjacencies.

\emph{Levels.} In cases 1, 2, 4 and 5 the child has level
$\level(\sigma)+1$; in cases 6 and 7 the child's level equals
$\level(\sigma)$. (OneLevel) holds in all cases.
\end{proof}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{$\textsc{MaubachSubdivide}(K,\sigma)$~\cite{mello2006}}
\label{alg:maubach}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $k\gets\type(\sigma)$;\ $\epsilon\gets\sigma[0,k]$;\ $\mathit{st}\gets[\,]$;\ $Q\gets(\sigma)$
\While{$Q\neq\varnothing$}
  \State $c\gets\mathrm{pop}(Q)$
  \If{$c$ not visited}
    \State mark $c$ visited; append $c$ to $\mathit{st}$
    \For{$i\in\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus\{\type(c)\}$} \Comment{facets of $c$ containing $\epsilon$}
      \State $c_a\gets$ the cell adjacent to $c$ across $\partial_i c$ (skip if none)
      \If{$\level(c_a)<\level(c)$}
        \State $\textsc{MaubachSubdivide}(K,c_a)$;\ $c_a\gets$ the cell adjacent to $c$ across $\partial_i c$
      \EndIf
      \State \textbf{if} $c_a$ not visited \textbf{then} push $c_a$ onto $Q$
    \EndFor
  \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State split $\epsilon$, with the new vertex at position $k$, in every cell of $\mathit{st}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label[theorem]{thm:maubach}
Let $K$ be a conforming mesh satisfying the Maubach invariants, and
$\sigma$ a cell. Then \Cref{alg:maubach} terminates, only splits cells of
level at most $\level(\sigma)$, and produces a conforming mesh satisfying
the Maubach invariants. It is consistent, alternating and
level-homogeneous.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By induction on $\ell=\level(\sigma)$. Let $\epsilon=\msplit(\sigma)$ and
$k=\type(\sigma)$. We claim that every cell pushed onto $Q$ has level
$\ell$, contains $\epsilon$ and has split edge $\epsilon$, and that no
cell of level $\ge\ell$ is split before the final line.

Let $c$ be such a cell, and $i\notin\{0,\type(c)\}$; the facet
$\partial_ic$ contains $\epsilon$. By \Cref{lem:direction}, the cell $c_a$
across it has level $\le\ell$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\level(c_a)=\ell$, then \Cref{lem:direction} gives
  $\msplit(c_a)=\epsilon$.
\item If $\level(c_a)=\ell-1$, the recursive call has argument level
  $\ell-1$. By the induction hypothesis it terminates, preserves the
  invariants, and splits only cells of level $\le\ell-1$. In particular it
  splits no visited or queued cell, so $c$ is still a cell of the mesh.
  By (MaubachB), $\partial_ic=\partial_j c_a$ with
  $j\in\{0,\type(c_a)\}$; this facet does not contain
  $\msplit(c_a)$. When $c_a$ is split, the facet passes whole to a child
  of level $\ell$, by case 6 or 7 of \Cref{lem:twocells}, and cells of
  level $\ell$ are never split during the recursion. The cell across
  $\partial_ic$ is now of level $\ell$, and the first case applies.
\end{itemize}
This proves the claim. Each recursive call strictly lowers the level of
its argument, and each call visits each cell at most once, so the
algorithm terminates.

When the loop ends, $\mathit{st}$ consists of cells of level $\ell$
containing $\epsilon$ and closed under adjacency across facets
containing $\epsilon$. Since $\st(\epsilon,K)$ is strongly connected
through such facets, $\mathit{st}=\st(\epsilon,K)$. All its cells have
split edge $\epsilon$ and level $\ell$ (consistency, level
homogeneity), so the final line is a single stellar subdivision
$(\epsilon,v)_k$ and the mesh remains conforming.

The new adjacent pairs are of three kinds:
\begin{itemize}
\item pairs of children of the same cell, covered by \Cref{lem:children};
\item pairs of children of two cells of $\mathit{st}$, covered by case 3
  of \Cref{lem:twocells};
\item pairs formed by a child and a cell $\sigma'\notin\mathit{st}$. Here
  $\sigma'$ is adjacent across a facet $\partial_p c$ with
  $p\in\{0,\type(c)\}$, so $\level(\sigma')\in\{\ell,\ell+1\}$ by
  \Cref{lem:direction}; this is covered by cases 1, 2, 4--7 of
  \Cref{lem:twocells}.
\end{itemize}
Finally, the weld vertex of a child sits at position $l=\type(\text{parent})$,
while its split edge occupies positions $0$ and $\type(\text{child})\ne l$.
The scheme is therefore alternating.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Geometry]
The scheme is purely combinatorial; where the new vertex is placed is a
separate choice. With midpoints on Kuhn's cube, cells of equal type are
similar~\cite{maubach1995}. In \Cref{sec:example} we place vertices at
Fubini--Study geodesic midpoints instead; the combinatorics is
unchanged.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Relation to Traxler and Stevenson}
\label{sec:stevenson}

Traxler~\cite{traxler1997} and Stevenson~\cite{stevenson2008} impose a
condition on the initial mesh only, and prove that all uniform
refinements are conforming~\cite[Thm.~4.3]{stevenson2008}. They use a
different convention. A tagged simplex $(x_0,\dots,x_d)_\gamma$ is
bisected along $x_0x_d$, and
$T_R=(x_d,x_1,\dots,x_\gamma,x_{d-1},\dots,x_{\gamma+1},x_0)_\gamma$ has
the same children. Two neighbors are \emph{reflected} if the vertex
sequence of $T$ or of $T_R$ agrees with that of $T'$ in all but one
position. Stevenson's condition~(b) asks that neighbors be reflected when
the refinement edge of either lies on the common facet, and that their
neighboring children be reflected otherwise. At level $0$, Traxler's type
$\gamma=0$ corresponds to Maubach's type $d$ with the same vertex
order~\cite[Rem.~4]{dgs2025}; in both conventions the refinement edge is
the first--last pair.

\begin{proposition}\label[proposition]{prop:stevenson}
Let $K$ be a level-$0$ mesh satisfying (MaubachA), and $\sigma,\omega$
adjacent.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt]
\item If $\partial_i\sigma=\partial_i\omega$, their vertex sequences
  agree in all but position $i$.
\item If $\partial_0\sigma=\partial_d\omega$, neither refinement edge lies
  on the common facet, and the children containing the common facet have
  vertex sequences that agree in all but one position.
\end{enumerate}
Hence (MaubachA) at level $0$ implies condition~(b) of Stevenson, using
$T$ and never $T_R$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) is immediate. For (b), write $\sigma=\langle a,f_1,\dots,f_d\rangle$ and
$\omega=\langle f_1,\dots,f_d,b\rangle$. The refinement edges $\{a,f_d\}$
and $\{f_1,b\}$ do not lie in $\{f_1,\dots,f_d\}$. The children
containing the common facet are
$c_1\sigma=\langle f_1,\dots,f_d,v_\sigma\rangle$ and
$c_0\omega=\langle f_1,\dots,f_d,v_\omega\rangle$, which differ only in the
last position.
\end{proof}

We do not know whether the converse holds: Stevenson's condition may
relate neighbors through $T_R$, i.e.\ with their common vertices in
reversed order, which equalities of ordered faces cannot express. By
Stevenson's Remark~4.4 his condition is necessary for conformity of all
uniform refinements, and by \Cref{thm:maubach} (MaubachA) at level $0$ is
sufficient. What \Cref{def:maubach} adds to these initial-mesh conditions
is heredity: it holds at every level, which is what makes the local,
inductive proof of \Cref{thm:maubach} possible.

\section{Initial meshes: strongly compatible seeds are balanced}
\label{sec:initial}
% =====================================================================

\Cref{thm:maubach} moves the whole difficulty to the initial mesh: once
the Maubach invariants hold at level $0$, refinement takes care of
itself. For products of simplices, such as Kuhn's cube, they hold by
construction; in general they do not. Our motivating application raises
the question for a seed that triangulates a closed $4$-manifold, $\CP^2$,
rather than a domain of $\R^d$. This section recalls that the strong
form of the invariant is balance, and uses this to bring in what is known
about balanced triangulations of closed manifolds.

\subsection{The equivalence}

\begin{definition}
A pure $d$-dimensional complex $K$ is \emph{balanced} if there is a map
$\col:V\to\{0,\dots,d\}$, a \emph{coloring}, that is injective on the
vertices of every cell. Equivalently, its $1$-skeleton is properly
$(d{+}1)$-colorable.
\end{definition}

The next proposition is a reformulation of Traxler's reflected
condition~\cite{traxler1997}. It is stated in terms of colorings
in~\cite[\S3.1]{dgs2025}. We include a proof in the present language.

\begin{proposition}\label[proposition]{prop:strong-balanced}
A conforming $d$-mesh admits a vertex order in every cell making it
strongly compatible if and only if it is balanced. The order of each
cell is then the order of the colors of its vertices, which is unique up to
a global permutation of the colors.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
($\Leftarrow$) Order each cell by color. The common facet of two adjacent
cells carries every color except the one omitted, which occupies the same
position $i$ in both cells, so $i=j$.
($\Rightarrow$) If $\partial_i\sigma=\partial_i\omega$ as ordered
simplices, every vertex of the common facet occupies the same position in
$\sigma$ and in $\omega$. The star of a vertex $v$ is strongly connected
through facets containing $v$, so the position of $v$ is the same in all
cells containing it. This position defines $\col(v)$, and it is injective
on each cell. Uniqueness follows by the same propagation argument.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Index-only matching is not enough]\label[remark]{rem:index-only}
A weaker, tempting condition asks only that the omitted vertex sit at the
same local \emph{index} on both sides of each facet, i.e.\ a proper
$(d{+}1)$-edge-coloring of the dual graph in which every cell sees every
color. It does not suffice. In dimension $3$, take
$\sigma=\langle a,b,c,d\rangle$ and $\omega=\langle d,x,c,a\rangle$,
sharing $\{a,c,d\}$ at index $1$ on both sides, with common split edge
$\{a,d\}$. After one bisection with new vertex $m$, the children
$\langle a,b,c,m\rangle$ and $\langle x,c,a,m\rangle$ share $\{a,c,m\}$
at indices $1$ and $0$, at type $2$. This violates (MaubachA): the split
edge $\{a,c\}$ of the first lies in the second, whose split edge is
$\{x,a\}$. Index-only matchings are much more common than balance.
By Kőnig's theorem they exist whenever the dual graph is bipartite, and
exhaustive search finds one on Kühnel's $\CP^2_9$, whose dual graph is not
bipartite and which is not balanced (\Cref{prop:kuhnel}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Classical constructions}

\begin{corollary}[{\cite[Thm.~9]{mello2006}}]\label[corollary]{cor:product}
The staircase triangulation of a product of simplices
$\Delta^{m_1}\times\cdots\times\Delta^{m_k}$ is balanced, with
$\col(v_L)=|L|$. In particular Kuhn's triangulation of the cube is
strongly compatible.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[{\cite[Thm.~10]{mello2006}}]\label[corollary]{cor:bary}
The barycentric subdivision of any mesh is balanced, with the barycenter
of $\delta$ colored $\dim\delta$. It is produced by the ordered stellar
subdivisions $(\delta,v_\delta)_{\dim\delta}$, in order of decreasing
dimension.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}\label[proposition]{prop:product}
If $K_1,K_2$ are balanced with colorings $\col_1,\col_2$, the staircase
triangulation of $K_1\times K_2$, built prism by prism from monotone
lattice paths, is balanced with $\col(a,b)=\col_1(a)+\col_2(b)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A cell of the prism $\sigma_1\times\sigma_2$ is a monotone path
$L_0,\dots,L_{m_1+m_2}$ with $|L_p|=p$. Its $p$-th vertex is
$(\sigma_1[L^1_p],\sigma_2[L^2_p])$, of color $L^1_p+L^2_p=p$. The coloring
is defined on vertices, so it is consistent across prisms.
\end{proof}

For instance, the octahedron is balanced (color $\pm e_k$ by $k$), so the
staircase triangulation of $S^2\times S^2$ as a product of two octahedra
is a strongly compatible seed.

Stevenson's pre-refinement~\cite[App.~A]{stevenson2008}, after
Kossaczký, is essentially the barycentric subdivision without the
edge-midpoint level: it has $\frac12(d{+}1)!$ cells per simplex instead
of $(d{+}1)!$, and satisfies the weaker matching condition. The coloring
initialization of~\cite{dgs2025} avoids pre-refinement altogether; for
balanced meshes it coincides with \Cref{prop:strong-balanced}, and for
the others it uses more than $d+1$ colors.

\subsection{Closed manifolds}
\label{sec:colorability}

For a closed $d$-manifold $M$, Dynnikov and Novikov consider three
classes of triangulations, which Gaifullin~\cite[\S2]{gaifullin2009}
denotes $T_{\mathrm{colour}}(M)\subseteq T_{\mathrm{even}}(M)$ and
$T_{\mathrm{bw}}(M)\subseteq T_{\mathrm{even}}(M)$:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item $T_{\mathrm{colour}}(M)$ consists of the balanced triangulations;
\item $T_{\mathrm{bw}}(M)$ consists of the triangulations whose top
  simplices admit a \emph{chess coloring}, i.e.\ whose dual graph is
  bipartite;
\item $T_{\mathrm{even}}(M)$ consists of the triangulations in which every
  $(d{-}2)$-simplex lies in an even number of top simplices.
\end{itemize}
Gaifullin shows that $T_{\mathrm{colour}}\subseteq T_{\mathrm{bw}}$ when
$M$ is orientable, and that all three classes coincide when $M$ is simply
connected. The first inclusion has a one-line proof in our language:

\begin{lemma}[{\cite[\S2]{gaifullin2009}}]\label[lemma]{lem:chess}
The dual graph of an orientable, strongly connected, balanced, closed
pseudomanifold is bipartite.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix a coherent orientation. Let $\varepsilon(\sigma)=\pm1$ record whether
the color order of $\sigma$ is positively oriented. Two adjacent cells
induce on their common facet the orientations $(-1)^k\varepsilon(\sigma)$
and $(-1)^k\varepsilon(\omega)$ relative to its color order, where $k$ is
the missing color. Coherence makes these opposite, so
$\varepsilon(\sigma)=-\varepsilon(\omega)$.
\end{proof}

Combined with \Cref{prop:strong-balanced}, this identifies the seeds that
our framework accepts.

\begin{corollary}\label[corollary]{cor:seeds}
Let $K$ be a triangulation of a closed combinatorial $d$-manifold $M$,
$d\ge2$. Then $K$ is a strongly compatible seed for Maubach bisection if
and only if $K\in T_{\mathrm{colour}}(M)$. If $M$ is simply connected,
this holds if and only if every $(d{-}2)$-simplex of $K$ has even degree.
\end{corollary}

The degree criterion is Traxler's condition for simply connected
domains~\cite{traxler1997}, now for closed manifolds. Joswig's
projectivity group gives the general criterion~\cite{joswig2002}.
A lower bound on the size of such seeds is also known:

\begin{proposition}[{\cite[Prop.~2.3]{gaifullin2009}}]\label[proposition]{prop:3d3}
A balanced combinatorial triangulation of a closed $d$-manifold with
fewer than $3d+3$ vertices triangulates a sphere.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Seeds for $\CP^2$}
\label{sec:cp2}

$\CP^2$ has two classical small triangulations. Kühnel's
$\CP^2_9$~\cite{kuhnelbanchoff1983} has $9$ vertices and is vertex-minimal.
Gaifullin's $X$~\cite{gaifullin2009} has $15$ vertices, $f$-vector
$(15,90,240,270,108)$, and automorphism group $S_4\times S_3$, which acts
by Fubini--Study isometries in Gaifullin's explicit realization.

\begin{proposition}\label[proposition]{prop:kuhnel}
Kühnel's $\CP^2_9$ is not a strongly compatible seed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\CP^2_9$ is $3$-neighborly, so its graph is $K_9$, which is not
$5$-colorable. Alternatively, it has only $9<15$ vertices, and
\Cref{prop:3d3} applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Gaifullin~\cite{gaifullin2009}]\label[theorem]{thm:gaifullin}
$X$ is balanced: color the three vertices of $V_4\setminus\{e\}$ with $0$
and the vertices $(a,b)$ with $a$, for $a=1,\dots,4$. It is a
vertex-minimal balanced triangulation of $\CP^2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label[corollary]{cor:minimal-seed}
Every strongly compatible seed of $\CP^2$ has at least $15$ vertices, and
Gaifullin's $X$, ordered by its coloring, is a strongly compatible seed
with exactly $15$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Combine \Cref{cor:seeds}, \Cref{prop:3d3} and \Cref{thm:gaifullin}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
We verified these facts independently on the facet list of $X$ (with
\texttt{simpcomp}~\cite{simpcomp}):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item every vertex has exactly two non-neighbors, namely the other members
  of its color class;
\item all $240$ triangles have degree $4$ or $6$;
\item in the color order, all $270$ shared facets satisfy $i=j$.
\end{itemize}
The $108$ seed cells are also geometrically uniform: the max/min
Fubini--Study edge ratio is $1.4636$ in every cell, as expected from the
isometric symmetry. By \Cref{cor:bary}, the barycentric subdivision of
$\CP^2_9$ is balanced too. In Kühnel's standard realization, however,
several flag barycenters coincide with existing vertices, which produces
degenerate cells.
\end{remark}

\section{Pointerless codes on balanced seeds}
\label{sec:lpt}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Why codes}
\label{sec:why-codes}

An explicit mesh stores each simplex with references to its faces and
cofaces. The number of faces per cell grows quickly with the dimension. In
the Freudenthal--Kuhn triangulation of $\R^d$, which is the combinatorial
type of uniform Maubach refinements of the cube, a $k$-simplex is
determined up to translation by an ordered sequence of $k$ disjoint
nonempty subsets of $\{1,\dots,d\}$. There are therefore
$k!\,S(d{+}1,k{+}1)/d!$ of them per $d$-cell, where $S$ denotes Stirling
numbers of the second kind. A full incidence structure storing only the
boundary references ($k+1$ integers per $k$-simplex) already needs:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
$d$ & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8 \\
\midrule
simplices of dim.\ $1..d$ per cell & 2.5 & 4.2 & 6.2 & 9.0 & 13.0 & 18.8 & 27.1 \\
boundary references per cell & 6.0 & 12.3 & 22.5 & 39.0 & 65.7 & 108.3 & 175.8 \\
LPT code bits (40 levels) & 49 & 52 & 55 & 59 & 62 & 66 & 70 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A \emph{compact} explicit structure stores cells only, each with its $d+1$
vertex identifiers and $d+1$ neighbor identifiers, i.e.\ $8(d+1)$ bytes
per cell with 32-bit integers ($40$ bytes for $d=4$) plus bookkeeping for
levels and the hierarchy. An LPT code needs about
$L+\log_2(2^dd!)+O(\log d)$ bits for $L$ levels, whatever the dimension.

We measured one full incidence structure, VGTL's
\texttt{nmt<4>}~\cite{vgtl}, against the pointerless leaf table of
\Cref{sec:leafset}, on uniform refinement of Gaifullin's seed with no
coordinates and no other data. Both hold exactly the same $108\cdot2^D$
leaves.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{\texttt{nmt<4>}} & & \multicolumn{2}{c}{pointerless} \\
\cmidrule{2-3}\cmidrule{5-6}
$D$ (leaves) & cells stored & bytes/leaf & & table & bytes/leaf \\
\midrule
8 ($27\,648$) & $55\,188$ & 222 & & 0.5 MB & 19.5 \\
12 ($442\,368$) & $884\,628$ & 222 & & 8.6 MB & 19.5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\texttt{nmt<4>} keeps every cell ever created, so that coarsening remains
possible, together with all faces; this costs $222$ bytes per leaf. The
pointerless table stores one 64-bit code per leaf at load factor $0.41$,
i.e.\ $19.5$ bytes per leaf. That is about $11\times$ less than this full
incidence structure, and about $2\times$ less than the $40$-byte compact
structure estimated above. Neither figure includes per-vertex data such
as coordinates, which both approaches need when geometry is required.
Beyond memory, the main benefit of the codes is that neighbors,
children and parents are computed rather than stored. This keeps the
data structure trivially consistent under refinement.

\subsection{LPT codes}

Atalay and Mount~\cite{atalaymount2007} encode the Maubach hierarchy of a
Kuhn-triangulated cube $[-1,1]^d$. Its reference root simplex is
\[
  S_\varnothing=\{x\in\R^d:\ 1\ge x_1\ge x_2\ge\dots\ge x_d\ge-1\},
\]
whose facet $H_0$ lies on $x_1=1$, facet $H_j$ ($0<j<d$) on $x_j=x_{j+1}$,
and facet $H_d$ on $x_d=-1$. A descendant is encoded by its \emph{LPT
code} $(\ell,\Pi,\Phi)$:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item $\ell$ is the level modulo $d$;
\item $\Pi=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_d)$ is a signed permutation;
\item $\Phi$ is the orthant list. After every $d$ levels, one bit is
  appended to each coordinate. We read these bits as integers
  $o_1,\dots,o_d\in[0,2^r)$, where $r$ is the number of completed rounds
  and the oldest bit is the most significant.
\end{itemize}
Their Theorem~1 gives the vertices explicitly. With $T_j=2^r-1-2o_j$, the
$k$-th vertex is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lpt-vertex}
  x^{(k)}=\frac{T+y^{(k)}}{2^r},\qquad
  y^{(k)}_{|\pi_j|}=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_j)\,v_{kj},\qquad
  v_{kj}=\begin{cases}1,&k\ge j,\\0,&k<\min(j,\ell),\\-1,&\ell\le k<j,\end{cases}
\end{equation}
for $k=0,\dots,d$ and $j=1,\dots,d$. Here $y^{(k)}\in\{-1,0,1\}^d$ depends
only on $(\ell,\Pi,k)$, while $T$ depends only on the orthant list.
Their Theorem~2 computes the code of a facet neighbor by table lookup.
It describes neighbors in the infinite Kuhn arrangement: in the cube, a
neighbor outside one root simplex is another root simplex of the same
cube. Our implementation descends from the one we wrote for the Adaptive
Voids algorithm~\cite{sa2015adaptive}, where LPT codes over the six Kuhn
tetrahedra of a cube drive an adaptive subdivision of a solid.

\subsection{Balanced seeds}

Let $K_0$ be a balanced $d$-dimensional seed with $N_0$ cells, each ordered
by color, and identify every seed cell $\tau=\langle\tau[0],\dots,\tau[d]\rangle$
with $S_\varnothing$ by sending the $k$-th vertex of $S_\varnothing$ to
$\tau[k]$. A cell of the refined seed is a pair $(s,\kappa)$: a seed index
$s$ and an LPT code $\kappa$ of the reference hierarchy.

We work \emph{combinatorially}: a vertex of the hierarchy of $\tau_s$ is
identified with its barycentric coordinates $w\in\Delta^d$ with respect to
$\tau_s$. These are dyadic rationals, and by \eqref{eq:lpt-vertex} the
coordinates $w(\kappa,m)$ of the $m$-th vertex of $(s,\kappa)$ depend on
$\kappa$ only. A vertex on a facet of $\tau_s$ is also a vertex of the
neighboring seed cell, via the identification of the common facet. A
geometric realization is applied afterwards: affine, or by Fubini--Study
midpoints as in \Cref{sec:example}. Since it is defined edge by edge from
the endpoints of the split edge, it respects these identifications.

\begin{proposition}[seed-boundary test]\label[proposition]{prop:boundary-test}
Facet $\partial_i$ of the cell with code $\kappa=(\ell,\Pi,\Phi)$ lies on
the facet $H_j$ of its seed cell if and only if:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=1pt]
\item\label{it:parallel} the linear part of the equation of $H_j$ is
  constant on $\{y^{(k)}:k\ne i\}$, i.e.\ $y^{(k)}_1\equiv1$ ($j=0$),
  $y^{(k)}_{j+1}-y^{(k)}_j\equiv2\delta$ with $\delta\in\{-1,0,1\}$
  ($0<j<d$), or $y^{(k)}_d\equiv-1$ ($j=d$); and
\item\label{it:offset} $o_1=0$ ($j=0$), $o_{j+1}-o_j=\delta$ ($0<j<d$), or
  $o_d=2^r-1$ ($j=d$).
\end{enumerate}
At most one $j$ satisfies \ref{it:parallel}, which depends only on
$(\ell,\Pi,i)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substitute \eqref{eq:lpt-vertex} into the equation of $H_j$. For $0<j<d$,
it reads $T_j+y^{(k)}_j=T_{j+1}+y^{(k)}_{j+1}$ for all $k\ne i$. Since $T$
does not depend on $k$, this forces $y^{(k)}_{j+1}-y^{(k)}_j$ to be
constant, equal to $T_j-T_{j+1}=2(o_{j+1}-o_j)$. For $j=0$, the equation
$T_1+y^{(k)}_1=2^r$ gives $2o_1=y^{(k)}_1-1$; since $o_1\ge0$ and
$y^{(k)}_1\le1$, this means $o_1=0$ and $y^{(k)}_1=1$. The case $j=d$ is
symmetric. At most one $j$ qualifies, because the $d$ vertices of a
facet span a hyperplane with a unique normal direction, and the facet
normals of a simplex are pairwise non-parallel.
\end{proof}

Condition~\ref{it:parallel} is precomputed in a table indexed by
$(\ell,\Pi,i)$ that stores $j$ and $\delta$, or ``none''. For $d=4$ the
table has $4\cdot384\cdot5=7\,680$ one-byte entries. The test is then a
single lookup followed by a single integer comparison.

\begin{proposition}[seed crossing]\label[proposition]{prop:crossing}
Suppose facet $\partial_i$ of $c=(s,\kappa)$ lies on the facet
$\partial_j\tau_s$ of its seed cell, and let $s'$ be the seed cell sharing
that facet. Then the neighbor of $c$ across $\partial_ic$ is
$c'=(s',\kappa)$, the same code in the other seed cell, and
$\partial_ic'=\partial_ic$ as ordered simplices.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{prop:strong-balanced}, $\partial_j\tau_s=\partial_j\tau_{s'}$
as ordered simplices, i.e.\ $\tau_s[p]=\tau_{s'}[p]$ for every $p\ne j$.
For $m\ne i$, the $m$-th vertex of $c$ lies on $\partial_j\tau_s$, so its
coordinates satisfy $w_j(\kappa,m)=0$. The $m$-th vertex of $c'$ has the
same coordinates with respect to $\tau_{s'}$, hence the same position on
the common facet. It is therefore the same vertex of the refined seed.
So $c'$ contains $\partial_ic$, in the same positions, has the same level
as $c$, and lies in the other seed cell. Uniform refinements of a
strongly compatible mesh are conforming (\Cref{thm:maubach}, applied to
every cell of a level in turn), so exactly two cells of that level
contain $\partial_ic$. Hence $c'$ is the neighbor.
\end{proof}

This is where balance, rather than any compatible ordering, is used:
without it the two identifications would differ on the common facet by a
permutation, and the code would have to be transformed.

\subsection{Neighbor finding}

\label{fig:seed-crossing}

To find the neighbor of $c=(s,\kappa)$ across $\partial_ic$:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Sibling facet.} If $\partial_ic$ is the facet created by the
  last bisection ($i=d$ for a $0$-child, $i=\type$ for a $1$-child),
  return the other child of the parent (\Cref{lem:children}).
\item \emph{Boundary test.} Otherwise, look up $(j,\delta)$ for
  $(\ell,\Pi,i)$ and check the orthant condition of
  \Cref{prop:boundary-test}.
\item \emph{Interior facet.} If the test fails, return $(s,\kappa')$ with
  $\kappa'$ given by Atalay and Mount's Theorem~2.
\item \emph{Seed crossing.} Otherwise return $(\adj[s][j],\kappa)$
  (\Cref{prop:crossing}), where $\adj$ is the $N_0\times(d{+}1)$ seed
  adjacency table. Root cells use this rule directly, with $j=i$.
\end{enumerate}
Rule~2 is indispensable. Across a seed-boundary facet, Atalay and Mount's
formula returns a cell of the reflected reference simplex, i.e.\ one with
negative barycentric weights. Such crossings are frequent: about $24\%$
of the non-sibling facets sampled at realistic depths lie on the seed
boundary. \Cref{fig:seed-crossing} illustrates rules~2 and~4.

\paragraph{Verification.}
We checked every returned neighbor \emph{exactly} on Gaifullin's seed
($d=4$). Both cells were written as integer barycentric coordinates over
the $15$ global vertices, with common denominator $2^{11}$, and we
checked that the returned cell is a different cell of the same level
containing the queried facet. The test covered $10^6$ random cells over
the whole representable depth range and five million queries: $977\,070$
sibling, $3\,317\,979$ interior and $704\,951$ seed-crossing. None failed,
and away from sibling facets the facet always sat at the same local index
on both sides. An earlier implementation computed the boundary test from
the full matrix of barycentric weights ($O(d^2+rd)$ integer operations)
and resolved crossings by floating-point point location. The table-based
test agrees with it on $7.8$ million facets, and same-code crossing agrees
on $1.4$ million crossings. The new rules take $4.3$\,ns and $5$\,ns per
query, against $79$\,ns and $211$\,ns.

\subsection{The leaf set}
\label{sec:leafset}

The mesh is the set of codes of the current leaves, stored in an
open-addressing hash table: one 64-bit slot per leaf, linear probing,
and backward-shift deletion, which avoids tombstones under the
erase-two-insert churn of bisection. For Gaifullin's seed a code fits in
one word: $7$ bits of seed index, $2$ bits for $\ell$, $9$ for $\Pi$, $4$
for $r$ and $4\times10$ for the orthant list, i.e.\ $62$ bits and a depth of
up to $43$. Two operations are built on the table:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item $\texttt{compat\_bisect}(c)$ is \Cref{alg:maubach} on codes. It
  bisects coarser neighbors recursively before bisecting $c$, so the mesh
  stays graded: no facet separates cells more than one level apart;
\item $\texttt{neighbor\_leaf}(c,i)$ walks up the ancestor chain of the
  combinatorial neighbor to the current leaf, or reports that the region
  has been refined further.
\end{itemize}
An exhaustive graded-invariant test, with about $40\,000$ cascading
bisections, $4$ million leaves and $11.6$ million facet checks, found no
violation. Vertex identifiers are not part of the code. When needed
(for geometry), a new vertex receives an integer identifier, keyed by the
identifiers of the endpoints of the split edge in a separate hash table;
vertices are about $1/d!$ as numerous as cells.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Enumerating the several finer leaves that border a coarser facet is not
implemented; such queries are reported, never guessed. Seeds that are
compatible only in the weak sense of (MaubachA) would need a permutation
correction in \Cref{prop:crossing}. For the initialization
of~\cite{dgs2025} with $N>d$ colors, a cell is a face of a virtual
$N$-simplex. Refinements of the virtual extension do not restrict to
refinements of the mesh~\cite[Rem.~12]{dgs2025}, so it is unclear how to
encode them.

\section{Example: plane curves inside $\CP^2$}
\label{sec:example}
% =====================================================================

Let $F(X,Y,Z)$ be homogeneous of degree $n$ and $C=\{F=0\}\subset\CP^2$
smooth. Then $C$ is a compact Riemann surface of genus $(n-1)(n-2)/2$, and a
real surface of codimension $2$ in $\CP^2$. We triangulate it as follows.

\paragraph{Mesh of $\CP^2$.}
The seed is Gaifullin's $X$ with his coordinates~\cite{gaifullin2009},
moved by a random unitary change of basis of $\C^3$ to avoid special
alignments between the curve and the seed. It is refined by pointerless
Maubach bisection (\Cref{sec:lpt}). Each new vertex is placed at the
Fubini--Study geodesic midpoint of the split edge, after aligning the
phases of the two homogeneous representatives. Coordinates are cached by
vertex identifier.

\paragraph{Extraction.}
For a leaf $c$, each of its $10$ triangular $2$-faces is intersected with
$C$ by Newton's method in the best-conditioned affine chart of that face.
Results are cached by the face's vertex identifiers, so adjacent cells
see identical crossings. On each tetrahedral facet, the crossings are
paired into arcs, by minimal total geodesic length when there are four.
The arcs are then assembled into closed polygons approximating $C\cap c$.
A cell is \emph{bad} when this assembly fails, e.g.\ at a node of degree
$\ne2$, which happens near tangencies.

\paragraph{Continuation.}
Starting from one seed cell that meets $C$, a frontier of cells is
processed as follows:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item a cell that meets $C$ and is below the target depth is bisected with
  \texttt{compat\_bisect}, and its new leaves join the frontier;
\item a cell at the target depth is extracted, and its neighbors across
  the five facets, found by \texttt{neighbor\_leaf}, join the frontier.
\end{itemize}
Moving through the mesh thus uses only the combinatorial operations of
\Cref{sec:lpt}. Two optional mechanisms refine further near tangencies:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item a \emph{tangency threshold} on a Cauchy--Riemann transversality
  score computed at each crossing;
\item \emph{repair rounds} that re-bisect bad cells.
\end{itemize}
The continuation reaches every cell whose $2$-faces are crossed by a
connected curve. A small closed component of $C\cap c$ that meets no
$2$-face of $c$ would be missed, and a reducible curve needs one starting
cell per component.

\paragraph{Topological consistency.}
The output is not certified. Its topology can nevertheless be checked,
directly on the extracted $2$-complex in $\CP^2$, independently of any
projection. Vertices are identified by the mesh vertex, edge or $2$-face
on which the crossing lies. Bad cells leave holes. We split each
\emph{pinched} vertex into one copy per component of its link. In all our
runs these vertices lie on hole boundaries, where a hole touches itself
or two holes meet. If the holes are discs, then
$g=(2c-b-\chi)/2$, where $c$ is the number of components and $b$ the number
of boundary loops. A closing step then turns the complex into a closed
surface:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item it keeps the main component;
\item it caps each hole by a cone from a new apex, placed at the loop's
  Fubini--Study barycenter and moved onto $C$ by Newton's method;
\item it checks that the result is a connected closed surface with
  $\chi=2-2g$.
\end{itemize}
These are necessary conditions. A missing handle and a spurious one would
cancel in $\chi$, and $\chi$ says nothing about the embedding. They are
nevertheless a useful sanity check for an uncertified method.
\Cref{tab:rotations} repeats the construction for five random rotations
of the seed. In all ten runs the genus estimate is exact, and the closed
surface passes every check. The bad-cell rate varies moderately with the
rotation. \Cref{app:measurements} gives further measurements: the
bad-cell rate against depth, a breakdown of the causes of failure, and the
accuracy of the extracted vertices.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

Stellar subdivision schemes reduce the correctness of Maubach bisection to
three local lemmas about ordered stellar operations. On conforming
meshes, the strong form of the invariant is balance. This connects the
choice of a seed with the classes of triangulations studied by
Dynnikov, Novikov and Gaifullin. For simply connected closed manifolds, a
triangulation is a strongly compatible seed exactly when all its
codimension-2 faces have even degree, and for $\CP^2$ Gaifullin's
triangulation is the smallest such seed. Balance is also what lets
pointerless LPT codes run over arbitrary seeds, with a table-driven,
floating-point-free neighbor procedure.

Several questions remain open:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item whether Stevenson's matching condition implies (MaubachA) after a
  suitable choice between $T$ and $T_R$;
\item pointerless codes for weakly compatible seeds and for the $N>d$
  initializations of~\cite{dgs2025};
\item small balanced seeds for other closed manifolds, for instance via
  cross-flips~\cite{ikn2017,venturello2019};
\item certification of the example's extraction.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Causes of failure.}
We instrumented the extraction at depth $14$ ($136\,786$ touched cells over
six curves, $8.0\%$ bad):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item $82\%$ of the bad cells fail on node degree, and $18\%$ on facets

\item cells with a facet touched at a single point are seven times more
  likely to be bad;
\item the transversality score separates good cells (median $0.21$) from
  bad ones (median $0.12$), with a monotone gradient in bad rate across
  score buckets.
\end{itemize}

At depth $20$ we evaluated $|F|$, with unit-norm homogeneous

is $1.0\times10^{-16}$, the $99$th percentile $4.3\times10^{-13}$ and the

distance between the polygons and the curve was not measured.

\paragraph{Mesh size.}

mesh reached depth $20$, with $6.8$ million leaves, on a machine with
$7.2$\,GB of RAM, where the \texttt{nmt<4>}-based implementation had
peaked at 4--5\,GB around depth $18$.
